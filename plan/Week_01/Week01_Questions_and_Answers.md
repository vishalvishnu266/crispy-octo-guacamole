## Day 1 – Historical Background & Making

**P1.1** The Regulating Act 1773 made the Governor of Bengal the Governor-General of Bengal (Warren Hastings was the first). The Charter Act 1833 made it Governor-General of India (Lord William Bentinck was the first).
⚠️ **Trap:** UPSC mixes up 1773 and 1833. Remember that "Bengal" comes first in 1773 and "India" in 1833.

**P1.2** (c) Secretary of State, 1858 → (b) separate electorate, 1909 → (a) provincial dyarchy, 1919 → (d) provincial autonomy, 1935
🧠 The sequence is: Crown rule (1858) → communal electorate (1909) → partial self-rule in the provinces (1919) → provincial autonomy (1935).

**P1.3** (i) Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha (the oldest member) (ii) Dr. Rajendra Prasad (elected 11 Dec 1946) (iii) Sir B.N. Rau. *Also: the Drafting Committee chairman was Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, and the Vice-Presidents were H.C. Mukherjee and V.T. Krishnamachari.*

**P1.4** About 250 provisions came from the 1935 Act: the **federal scheme**, the **office of Governor**, the **judiciary**, the **Public Service Commissions**, the **emergency provisions**, and administrative details. The three lists (Federal, Provincial, Concurrent) became our Union, State and Concurrent Lists.

**P1.5** It was set up under the Cabinet Mission Plan (1946) with **389 seats**: 296 for British India (292 from provinces and 4 from Chief Commissioner's provinces) and 93 for the princely states. British India's members were **indirectly elected** by the provincial assemblies through proportional representation by single transferable vote. Princely state members were **nominated** by the rulers. Seats were allotted roughly at 1 per 10 lakh population. After Partition, membership fell to **299**.
⚠️ It was **not directly elected** and **not based on adult franchise**. This is a common UPSC trap.

**P1.6** **(d) All four.**
1 ✓ Cabinet Mission Plan, 1946. 2 ✓ It acted as the Dominion Legislature (the first Parliament) after the 1947 Independence Act. 3 ✓ The Muslim League boycotted it. 4 ✓ The flag was adopted on 22 July 1947.
🧠 Other dates worth knowing: the National Anthem and National Song were adopted on 24 Jan 1950, and Dr. Rajendra Prasad was elected first President on the same day.

**P1.7** **(b) Only two** (1 and 3 are correct).
1 ✓ The 1813 Act ended the monopoly except for tea and the China trade.
2 ✗ The 1833 Act **attempted** open competition, but it was negated after opposition from the Court of Directors. Open competition was actually introduced by the **Charter Act 1853**.
3 ✓ The 1919 Act introduced bicameralism at the Centre.
4 ✗ The 1935 Act **abolished** provincial dyarchy and provided for dyarchy at the **Centre**, which was never implemented. Provincial dyarchy came in 1919.
⚠️ Watch for verbs like "attempted" vs "introduced". UPSC builds traps on them.

**P1.8** **(b) 1, 2 and 3 only.** The provisions on citizenship, elections, the provisional Parliament, and temporary and transitional matters came into force on 26 Nov 1949. The rest, including Fundamental Rights, took effect on 26 Jan 1950.

**P1.9** At the Lahore Session (Dec 1929, presided over by Nehru), the INC adopted the Purna Swaraj resolution, and **26 January 1930** was celebrated as Independence Day. The Constituent Assembly chose 26 January 1950 to honour that date.

**P1.10**
*Supporting:* (1) It borrowed heavily from the 1935 Act, about 250 provisions. (2) Members were indirectly elected on a limited franchise and dominated by Congress, and lawyers and politicians were over-represented.
*Countering:* (1) The Assembly included nearly every section of society: women, SCs, minorities, and people from every province. (2) Borrowed provisions were adapted to Indian conditions. The FRs, DPSPs, adult franchise and parliamentary system were not in the 1935 Act. (3) Decisions were taken by consensus after detailed debate over almost 3 years, with 11 sessions and public input on the draft.

**P1.11**
(a) **False.** The idea was first put forward by **M.N. Roy in 1934**. The INC officially demanded it in **1935**, and Nehru voiced it in 1938.
(b) **False.** It took **2 years, 11 months and 18 days**.
(c) **False.** The 1909 Act introduced **indirect** elections and separate electorates. Direct elections came with the **1919 Act**.

## Day 2 – Salient Features & Preamble

**P2.1** (a) DPSP: Ireland (b) Concurrent List: Australia (c) Procedure established by law: Japan (d) Fundamental Duties: USSR (e) Residuary powers with the Centre: Canada (f) Amendment procedure: South Africa

**P2.2** Once, by the **42nd Amendment (1976)**, which added **Socialist, Secular and Integrity**.

**P2.3** **Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic**.
🧠 Memory hook: "**S S S D R**".

**P2.4**
- **Berubari Union case (1960):** the Preamble is **not** a part of the Constitution.
- **Kesavananda Bharati (1973):** the Preamble **is** a part of the Constitution and can be amended, but its basic features cannot be destroyed.
- **LIC of India (1995)** reaffirmed that it is part of the Constitution.
🧠 It is part of the Constitution but non-justiciable, and it is neither a source of power nor a limitation on power.

**P2.5**
*Federal features:* two levels of government, division of powers (7th Schedule), a written and rigid constitution, an independent judiciary, bicameralism.
*Unitary features:* single citizenship, a single integrated judiciary, All-India Services, Governors appointed by the Centre, emergency provisions, Parliament's power to change state boundaries (Art 3), a strong Centre (residuary powers), unequal representation of states in the Rajya Sabha.
🧠 K.C. Wheare called it "quasi-federal". Ambedkar said it isn't bound into a "tight mould of federalism". The Supreme Court in *S.R. Bommai* (1994) held federalism to be part of the basic structure.

**P2.6** **(c) Only three** (1, 2 and 4).
3 ✗ The ideals of **justice (social, economic, political)** come from the **Russian Revolution**. **Liberty, equality and fraternity** come from the **French Revolution**.
⚠️ This Russian vs French distinction is a classic trap.

**P2.7** **(b) 2 and 3 only.** The Preamble is **neither a source of power nor a prohibition** on the legislature.

**P2.8**
- The challenge failed because the words had been part of the Constitution for almost 50 years, Parliament's amending power (Art 368) extends to the Preamble, and the words reflect values already present in the Constitution: Arts 25–28 for secularism and Art 38/39 (DPSP) for socialism. Being inserted in 1976 doesn't make them invalid. *(Dr. Balram Singh v. Union of India, Nov 2024)*
- **Indian secularism** is "positive": the State keeps an equal distance from all religions but can intervene to reform them (e.g. temple entry, banning untouchability). **Western secularism** is a strict wall of separation between Church and State.
- **Indian socialism** is **democratic socialism** (a mixed economy), not communist or state-owned socialism.

**P2.9** It does **not** compromise sovereignty. The Commonwealth is a **voluntary association** of nations. India accepted the British monarch only as the **symbol** of this association (the London Declaration, 1949). There are no legal obligations, India can leave at any time, and the monarch has no role in India's governance.

**P2.10**
(a) **False.** "Union of States" appears in **Article 1**, not in the Preamble.
(b) **False.** The Preamble **can** be amended, and it was amended in 1976. What can't be amended is its basic features.
(c) **True.** Liberty "of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship" is in the Preamble.

## Day 3 – Union & Territory, Citizenship

**P3.1**
- **Art 1:** "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States". It covers the name and territory.
- **Art 2:** Parliament can admit into the Union, or establish, **new States not already part of India**.
- **Art 3:** formation of new States, and alteration of the areas, boundaries or names of existing States.
- **Art 4:** laws made under Arts 2 and 3 are **not** amendments under Art 368.

**P3.2** **Andhra State, 1953.** It was formed after Potti Sriramulu died following a 56-day fast. The **Fazl Ali Commission** (States Reorganisation Commission, 1953–55) led to the **States Reorganisation Act, 1956**.
🧠 Earlier, the **Dhar Commission** (1948) rejected the linguistic principle, and the **JVP Committee** (Jawaharlal, Vallabhbhai, Pattabhi Sitaramayya, 1948–49) also opposed it.

**P3.3** **Acquiring citizenship:** birth, descent, registration, naturalisation, incorporation of territory. **Losing it:** renunciation, termination (automatic, when a person voluntarily acquires another citizenship), deprivation (by the government).

**P3.4** The bill can be introduced only **on the President's recommendation**. Before recommending it, the President refers it to the affected State legislature for its views within a set time. **Those views are not binding** on the President or Parliament. The bill is then passed by a **simple majority**. (For UTs, no such reference is needed.)

**P3.5** **Art 2** covers areas **outside** India being admitted or established (e.g. **Sikkim**, which became a state in 1975 via the 36th Amendment; Sikkim had earlier been an associate state under the 35th). **Art 3** covers **reorganising existing** states (e.g. Telangana, 2014; Andhra State, 1953).

**P3.6** **(d) 1, 2 and 3.**
3 ✓ The **Berubari case (1960)** held that ceding territory needs an amendment under Art 368. That led to the **9th Amendment (1960)**, and later the **100th Amendment (2015)** for the India–Bangladesh Land Boundary Agreement.
⚠️ However, **settling a boundary dispute** doesn't require an amendment, because it can be done by executive action (SC, 1969).

**P3.7** **(b)**
🧠 The citizenship-by-birth timeline:
- 26 Jan 1950 to 1 Jul 1987: born in India is enough, regardless of the parents
- 1 Jul 1987 to 3 Dec 2004: born in India and one parent is a citizen
- After 3 Dec 2004: born in India and both parents are citizens, or one is a citizen and the other is **not an illegal migrant**

**P3.8** **(b) Only two** (1 and 2).
3 ✗ The CAA **exempts** the tribal areas under the 6th Schedule (in Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram and Tripura) and the **Inner Line Permit** areas. The six communities are Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi and Christian. The residency requirement for naturalisation was cut from **11 years to 5**. The CAA Rules were notified in **March 2024**.

**P3.9** In the US, states existed before the Union and have territorial integrity: no state's boundary can change without its consent. In India, **Parliament can unilaterally change** state boundaries, names, or even existence by simple majority (Art 3), while the **Union itself is indestructible**: no state can secede.
**Example:** the J&K Reorganisation Act 2019 converted a **State into two Union Territories** (J&K and Ladakh) by an ordinary law. This shows how "destructible" the states are. (In 2023, the Supreme Court upheld the abrogation of Art 370 and asked for statehood to be restored "at the earliest".)

**P3.10** OCI is **not** dual citizenship. The Constitution doesn't allow dual citizenship (Art 9 and the Citizenship Act). An OCI cardholder **cannot**: (1) vote, (2) hold constitutional posts such as President, VP, or judge of the SC or HC, (3) normally hold government jobs, or (4) buy agricultural land. They get a lifelong visa and parity with NRIs in some economic matters.

**P3.11**
(a) **False.** A simple majority is enough (Art 3 and Art 4).
(b) **False.** Art 19 is for **citizens only**.
(c) **True.**

## Day 4 – Fundamental Rights (12–24)

**P4.1** Art 14: equality before law and equal protection of laws. Art 15: no discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth. Art 16: equality of opportunity in public employment. Art 17: abolition of untouchability. Art 18: abolition of titles. Art 20: protection in respect of conviction for offences. Art 21A: right to education. Art 23: prohibition of trafficking and forced labour. Art 24: prohibition of child labour (under 14, in factories, mines and hazardous work).

**P4.2** The **Right to Property** (Art 19(1)(f) and Art 31) was removed by the **44th Amendment (1978)**. It became a **legal right under Art 300A** (Part XII).

**P4.3** The **86th Amendment (2002)**, covering ages **6 to 14 years**. It is implemented through the RTE Act, 2009.

**P4.4** **Equality before law** (from Britain) is a **negative** concept: no special privileges for anyone, and everyone is equally subject to ordinary law. **Equal protection of laws** (from the USA) is a **positive** concept: equal treatment in equal circumstances, which allows **reasonable classification**.
🧠 The reasonable classification test is (1) intelligible differentia and (2) a rational nexus with the objective. The arbitrariness test comes from *E.P. Royappa* (1974).

**P4.5** In *A.K. Gopalan* (1950), "procedure established by law" was read literally. **Maneka Gandhi (1978)** held that the procedure must be **"fair, just and reasonable"**, which brought in **due process** in substance. It also linked Arts 14, 19 and 21 (the "golden triangle").
Rights read into Art 21 include privacy (2017), livelihood, a clean environment, a speedy trial, free legal aid, shelter, health, dignity in death (passive euthanasia, 2018), and sleep.

**P4.6** **(b) 2, 3 and 4 only.** Art 14 and Art 21 are available to **all persons**, including foreigners.

**P4.7** **(c) All three.** Art 20 also protects against **self-incrimination**. Under the 44th Amendment, **Arts 20 and 21** cannot be suspended even during an emergency.

**P4.8** **(c) 1, 2, 3 and 4.** All of them can be enforced against private persons.
🧠 In *Kaushal Kishor* (2023), the Supreme Court held that **Arts 19 and 21** can also be enforced against non-state actors, which extends horizontal application.

**P4.9**
(i) In *Indra Sawhney* (1992), the Supreme Court capped reservation at **50%**. EWS takes it beyond that. The majority in *Janhit Abhiyan* said the 50% ceiling is **not inflexible** and applies to backward-class reservations only.
(ii) Excluding **SC/ST/OBC** from EWS was called discriminatory by the minority judges, but the majority held it was a permissible classification.
⚠️ The verdict was **3:2**.
🧠 EWS was the **first reservation based purely on economic criteria**. It added Arts 15(6) and 16(6).

**P4.10** *K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017, a 9-judge bench) made privacy an intrinsic part of Art 21.
(1) **Aadhaar**: in *Puttaswamy-II* (2018), the Court upheld Aadhaar but struck down its mandatory use by private entities. (2) It led to the **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023**, and its **Rules (notified 2025)**. Privacy also comes up in debates on surveillance (Pegasus) and in the decriminalisation of Section 377 (*Navtej Johar*, 2018).

**P4.11**
(a) **False.** Under **Art 358**, Art 19 is suspended automatically only when the emergency is declared on grounds of **war or external aggression**, not **armed rebellion** (44th Amendment).
(b) **False.** National awards aren't "titles" under Art 18 (*Balaji Raghavan*, 1996). They shouldn't be used as prefixes or suffixes to names, though.
(c) **False.** A company isn't a citizen, so it can't claim Art 19. Its **shareholders** can, however.

## Day 6 – DPSP, FD, Amendment, Basic Structure

**P6.1** **Part IV, Arts 36–51.** **Article 37** says they are not enforceable by courts but are "fundamental in the governance of the country".

**P6.2** The 42nd Amendment added: **Art 39(f)** (opportunities for the healthy development of children), **Art 39A** (equal justice and free legal aid), **Art 43A** (workers' participation in the management of industries), and **Art 48A** (protection of the environment, forests and wildlife).
🧠 Other additions: the 44th added **Art 38(2)** (minimising inequalities in income), the 86th changed **Art 45**, and the 97th added **Art 43B** (cooperative societies).

**P6.3** The **Swaran Singh Committee (1976)**. It recommended 8, but the 42nd Amendment added **10**. There are now **11**. The 11th, **Art 51A(k)** (parents or guardians to provide educational opportunities for children aged 6–14), was added by the **86th Amendment (2002)**.

**P6.4**
1. **Champakam Dorairajan (1951):** FRs prevail over DPSPs, which led to the **1st Amendment**.
2. **Golaknath (1967):** Parliament **cannot amend FRs** to implement DPSPs.
3. **24th Amendment (1971):** Parliament can amend any part, including FRs. **25th Amendment (1971):** inserted **Art 31C**, so that laws implementing Art 39(b) and (c) can't be challenged under Arts 14 and 19.
4. **Kesavananda Bharati (1973):** Parliament can amend FRs but **cannot alter the basic structure**. The Court struck down the part of 31C that excluded judicial review.
5. **42nd Amendment (1976)** extended 31C to **all** DPSPs. **Minerva Mills (1980)** struck that down, holding that the **harmony and balance between FRs and DPSPs is part of the basic structure**.

**P6.5**
- **Simple majority (outside Art 368):** e.g. creating new States (Art 3), creating or abolishing Legislative Councils (Art 169), the 5th and 6th Schedules, citizenship, quorum, salaries.
- **Special majority** (a majority of total membership **and** 2/3 of members present and voting, in each House): e.g. FRs and DPSPs.
- **Special majority plus ratification by half the States** (by simple majority): federal provisions such as the election of the President, the extent of Union and State executive power, the SC and HCs, the distribution of legislative powers (7th Schedule), GST Council matters, the representation of States in Parliament, and Art 368 itself.

**P6.6**
| Art | Content | Category |
|---|---|---|
| 39A | Equal justice and free legal aid | **S** (Socialist) |
| 40 | Village panchayats | **G** (Gandhian) |
| 44 | Uniform Civil Code | **L** (Liberal-Intellectual) |
| 47 | Nutrition, public health, prohibition of intoxicants | **S**/**G**: improving nutrition and public health is socialist, while prohibition of intoxicating drinks is Gandhian. Laxmikanth places "prohibition" under Gandhian |
| 48 | Organising agriculture and animal husbandry on modern lines; banning cow slaughter | Modern agriculture is **L**, while banning cow slaughter is **G** |
| 50 | Separating the judiciary from the executive | **L** |

⚠️ Arts 47 and 48 have **two parts that fall in different categories**. This is a good example of why you should read the exact text of each DPSP.

**P6.7** **(b) Only two** (2 and 3).
1 ✗ An amendment bill can be introduced in **either House**.
4 ✗ **No prior permission** of the President is needed, and a private member can also introduce one.
3 ✓ The 24th Amendment made **assent compulsory**.

**P6.8** **(a) 1, 2 and 4 only.** Fundamental Rights need only a **special majority**, without state ratification.

**P6.9** Art 44 says "The State **shall endeavour** to secure a UCC throughout the territory of India." Marriage, divorce, succession and similar subjects are in the **Concurrent List (Entry 5)**, so **both Parliament and the States** can legislate on them. **Uttarakhand** passed its UCC Act in 2024 and implemented it on **27 Jan 2025**, becoming the first state after Independence to do so. **Goa** has had a civil code since Portuguese times. If a central law conflicts with a state law, the central law generally prevails (Art 254).

**P6.10** Simultaneous elections for the **Lok Sabha and State Assemblies** involve amending Arts 82A (new), 83 and 172. The government's view, following the Kovind Committee (2024), is that these need only a **special majority**. **Local body** elections fall under the **State List and Parts IX/IXA**, which concern the states. So the committee said syncing them would need **ratification by at least half the states**.
🧠 This is a good example of linking a current-affairs item to Art 368 (static).

**P6.11**
*For:* unelected judges can strike down constitutional amendments passed by elected representatives with a special majority, and the doctrine isn't written in the text.
*Against:* the doctrine protects the Constitution from **majoritarian excess**. It came after the Emergency-era amendments (the 39th and 42nd). It applies only to the **core identity** of the Constitution, and Parliament still amends most provisions freely.
**NJAC (2015):** the Court struck down the **99th Amendment** and the NJAC Act on the ground of **judicial independence** (a basic feature) and restored the **collegium** system. Critics call this judicial overreach.

**P6.12**
(a) **False.** They are **non-justiciable**, though courts may use them when interpreting laws.
(b) **False.** The basic structure isn't defined anywhere in the Constitution. It is a **judicial creation** that has evolved case by case.
(c) **False.** Fundamental Duties apply to **citizens only**.

## History Block 2 – Administration, Economy, Land Revenue

**H2.1**
(a) **Permanent Settlement:** Lord Cornwallis, **1793**. Covered Bengal, Bihar, Orissa, Varanasi, and the northern districts of Madras.
(b) **Ryotwari:** **Thomas Munro and Alexander Read**, around the 1820s. Covered Madras, Bombay, parts of Assam, and Coorg.
(c) **Mahalwari:** **Holt Mackenzie, 1822**, later revised under Bentinck (1833, by R.M. Bird and James Thomason). Covered the North-Western Provinces (UP), Punjab, and the Central Provinces.

**H2.2** (a) **Dadabhai Naoroji** (b) **R.C. Dutt**

**H2.3** **Lord Cornwallis** (the Cornwallis Code, 1793, which separated revenue and judicial administration). **Warren Hastings** (1772) set up the post of district Collector and the Diwani (civil) and Faujdari (criminal) Adalats.

**H2.4**
| Feature | Permanent (Zamindari) | Ryotwari | Mahalwari |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unit / settled with | Zamindar | Individual ryot (cultivator) | Village community (mahal) |
| Ownership | Zamindar | Ryot (but the state was the "supreme landlord") | Village body jointly |
| Revenue | **Fixed permanently** | Periodically revised (20–30 yrs) | Periodically revised |
| Coverage | about 19% | about 51% | about 30% |
| Impact | Absentee landlordism, rack-renting, tenant insecurity | Very high rates, debt, dependence on moneylenders | Power went to village headmen; high rates |

**H2.5** The Drain of Wealth was the transfer of India's wealth to Britain **without any equivalent economic return**. Its channels: **Home Charges** (salaries and pensions of British officials, India Office expenses), **interest on public debt**, **profits of British companies and investors**, **military expenditure** (including wars fought outside India), and **excess exports over imports** (an unrequited surplus).
🧠 Naoroji estimated the drain at about £12 million a year. The theory became the economic foundation of Indian nationalism.

**H2.6** **(c) Only three** (1, 2 and 3).
4 ✗ It was introduced in **Bengal**. Madras had the Ryotwari system.
🧠 The state's share was fixed at **10/11th (about 89%)** of the rent, and the zamindar kept **1/11th**.

**H2.7** **(b) 1 and 3 only.**
2 ✗ Shifting to cash crops **reduced food security** and contributed to famines.

**H2.8**
- **Mercantile phase (1757–1813):** monopoly trade and direct plunder of Bengal. Indian goods were bought cheap with Bengal's own revenue.
- **Free trade / industrial capital phase (1813–1858):** after the Charter Act 1813, British machine-made goods flooded India. India became a market for finished goods and a supplier of raw materials. Indian handicrafts collapsed through **one-way free trade** (high duties on Indian goods in Britain, low duties on British goods in India).
- **Finance imperialism (1860s onwards):** British capital was invested in railways, plantations and banks, and the profits went back to Britain.
- **Result:** the artisans who were displaced moved into agriculture, which increased pressure on land. Modern industry came only late and in a limited way.

**H2.9**
(a) **True.** Ryotwari covered about 51%.
(b) **False.** The government's share was about **89% (10/11th)**.
(c) **False.** Mahalwari was introduced by **Holt Mackenzie** (1822). Cornwallis introduced the Permanent Settlement.

**H3.4** **Reformist** movements responded to the times through reason, conscience and science, and were open to Western ideas. Examples: Brahmo Samaj, Prarthana Samaj, Aligarh Movement. **Revivalist** movements sought to revive ancient traditions and saw Western thought as corrupting Indian culture. Examples: Arya Samaj ("Back to the Vedas"), Deoband Movement.
⚠️ Many movements had both elements. The Arya Samaj was revivalist in its theology but reformist on caste, women's education and widow remarriage.

**H3.5** These were the **earliest centres of British rule and English education** (Calcutta, Bombay, Madras). They had an **emerging English-educated middle class**, the **printing press and newspapers**, and contact with Western liberal ideas and Christian missionaries.

**H3.6** **(c) All three.**
🧠 The Tattvabodhini Sabha was founded in **1839**, the split happened in **1866**, and the Sadharan Brahmo Samaj was formed in **1878**. The *Native Marriage Act 1872* (also called the Brahmo Marriage Act) prohibited child marriage among Brahmos, which is why Keshub's own daughter's marriage caused the split.

**H3.7** **(b) Only two** (1 and 2).
3 ✗ Sati was abolished in **1829 under Lord William Bentinck** (Regulation XVII), with Rammohan Roy's campaign behind it. Dalhousie was Governor-General from 1848 to 1856.

**H3.8**
*It contributed to nationalism by:* fostering **rational thinking and pride** in India's heritage (Vivekananda, Arya Samaj), attacking caste and gender divisions so that Indians could unite, creating a **public sphere** (press, associations, debates), and producing future national leaders. Lajpat Rai came from the Arya Samaj, and Annie Besant from the Theosophical Society led the Home Rule movement.
*Limitations:* (1) the movements were mostly **urban, upper-caste and middle-class**, and barely reached the masses; (2) they often **glorified the past** and contributed to religious identity and communal consciousness (e.g. the Arya Samaj's shuddhi vs Muslim tabligh); (3) they relied on **colonial legislation**; (4) lower-caste movements (Phule, Narayana Guru, Periyar) emerged because the upper-caste reformers didn't go far enough.

**H3.9**
(a) **False.** It was founded in **New York (1875)**. Its headquarters later moved to **Adyar (Madras)**.
(b) **False.** The Arya Samaj was founded at **Bombay (1875)**. Its main stronghold later became Lahore and Punjab.
