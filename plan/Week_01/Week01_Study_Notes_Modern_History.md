# 📗 BLOCK 1 (Mon–Tue) – ADVENT OF EUROPEANS & BRITISH CONQUEST

## ⏱ Snapshot
- **Arrival order (by company): Portuguese (1498) → Dutch (1602 VOC) → English (1600 EIC charter; arrived 1608) → Danes (1616) → French (1664).**
  🧠 "**P**lease **D**on't **E**at **D**ry **F**ood". ⚠️ The **EIC was formed in 1600, before the Dutch VOC (1602)**, but the **Dutch reached India first** (1605 Masulipatnam vs the English at Surat in 1608/1613).
- The **Anglo-French rivalry** was decided in the **three Carnatic Wars**. **Wandiwash (1760)** was decisive.
- **Bengal:** **Plassey (1757)** brought political control through conspiracy. **Buxar (1764)** was a military victory and led to the **Diwani (1765)**.
- **Expansion tools:** war → **Ring Fence** (Warren Hastings) → **Subsidiary Alliance** (Wellesley) → **Doctrine of Lapse** (Dalhousie) → annexation on the **pretext of misgovernance** (Awadh 1856).

## 1.1 The European companies

| Point | Detail |

| Pioneer | **Vasco da Gama** reached **Calicut (May 1498)** via the Cape of Good Hope, helped by a Gujarati pilot (traditionally called **Abdul Majid**). Received by the **Zamorin** (ruler of Calicut). His cargo sold for about **60 times** the cost of the voyage |
| 2nd voyage | **Pedro Álvares Cabral** (1500) |

| Legacy | **Tobacco** and potato cultivation (also chillies, cashew, pineapple via trade); the **first printing press in India (Goa, 1556)**; Gothic architecture in Goa; **Bombay** given to **Charles II of England** as dowry (**1661**, marriage to Catherine of Braganza) |
| End | Goa, Daman and Diu liberated by **Operation Vijay (Dec 1961)**. 🔗 Polity: 12th Amendment |

### Dutch
- **VOC (Dutch East India Company), 1602**.
- **First factory: Masulipatnam (1605)**. Others at **Pulicat (1610)**, Surat (1616), Bimlipatam, Karaikal, **Chinsura**, Kasimbazar, Baranagore, Patna, Balasore, **Nagapatnam**, Cochin.
- **HQ:** first **Pulicat**, later **Nagapatnam (1690)**.
- Focus: the **spice trade** of Indonesia (the Malay archipelago); **textiles from India** were their main export.
- **Battle of Amboyna (1623):** the Dutch massacred English traders in Indonesia, so the English turned to India.
- **Battle of Colachel (1741):** **Marthanda Varma of Travancore** defeated the Dutch, **the first defeat of a European naval power by an Asian kingdom** (an often-cited claim).
- **Battle of Bedara/Chinsurah (1759):** the **English defeated the Dutch**, ending Dutch ambitions in India.

## 1.2 Anglo-French Rivalry: The Carnatic Wars 📌📌

| | First (1740–48) | Second (1749–54) | Third (1756/58–63) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cause** | Extension of the **Austrian War of Succession** in Europe | **Succession disputes**: in **Hyderabad** (Nasir Jung vs Muzaffar Jung) and the **Carnatic** (Anwaruddin vs Chanda Sahib). French backed Muzaffar and Chanda; English backed Nasir and Anwaruddin, later Muhammad Ali | Extension of the **Seven Years' War** (1756–63) |
| **Key events** | French under Dupleix **captured Madras (1746)**. **Battle of St. Thome (1746)** on the **Adyar river**: a **small French force under Captain Paradis defeated the large army of Nawab Anwaruddin** | **Battle of Ambur (1749)**: Anwaruddin killed. **Robert Clive's siege of Arcot (1751)** turned the tide. The French Govt **recalled Dupleix (1754)** and replaced him with Godeheu | **Count de Lally** led the French. **Battle of Wandiwash (22 Jan 1760)**: **Sir Eyre Coote** defeated Lally. **Pondicherry fell (1761)** |
| **Result** | **Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748)**: **Madras returned** to the English (in exchange for Louisbourg in North America) | **Treaty of Pondicherry (1754/55)**: both agreed not to interfere in native quarrels | **Treaty of Paris (1763)**: French factories returned **but they could not fortify them** or keep troops. **End of French political ambitions** |
| **Lesson** | **A small disciplined European army could defeat large Indian armies** | Showed the European technique of **making puppet rulers** | The **English became the dominant European power** |

### Why the English won over the French 📌 (Mains-favourite)
1. **Nature of company:** the English EIC was a **private company** (enterprise, flexibility, its own finances). The French company was **state-controlled** and dependent on a government busy with European wars.
2. **Naval superiority**: the Royal Navy kept supply lines open and cut off the French.
3. **Financial strength**: after **Plassey (1757)** the English had **Bengal's revenue**. The French depended on the Deccan, whose resources were drained.
4. **Three strategic bases** (Calcutta, Bombay, Madras) vs the French concentration on Pondicherry.
5. **Better leadership**: Clive, Lawrence, Coote vs a recalled Dupleix and a quarrelsome Lally.
6. **Europe's priority**: France was more interested in continental European ambitions; Britain focused on trade and colonies.

🔗 **Wandiwash (1760) decided the Anglo-French struggle. Plassey (1757) gave the English the resources to win it.**

## 1.3 Conquest of Bengal 📌📌

### Bengal's nawabs (sequence)
**Murshid Quli Khan** (1717–27): de facto independent; shifted the capital from **Dacca to Murshidabad** → **Shuja-ud-din** → **Sarfaraz Khan** → **Alivardi Khan** (1740–56) → **Siraj-ud-Daulah** (1756–57) → **Mir Jafar** (1757–60) → **Mir Kasim** (1760–63) → **Mir Jafar** again (1763–65) → **Najm-ud-Daulah** (1765–66)

### Battle of Plassey (23 June 1757)
| Causes | Event | Results |
|---|---|---|
| • **Misuse of dastaks** (free passes) by Company servants for their **private trade**, which hurt Bengal's revenue<br>• **Fortification of Calcutta** without permission<br>• **Shelter to Krishna Das** (a fugitive)<br>• Siraj **captured Calcutta (June 1756)**. The "**Black Hole tragedy**" (Holwell's account) is disputed by historians<br>• **Treaty of Alinagar (Feb 1757)** after Clive and Watson recaptured Calcutta | **Clive vs Siraj** at Plassey (on the **Bhagirathi**). **Conspiracy**: **Mir Jafar** (commander), **Rai Durlabh**, **Jagat Seth** (banker), **Omichund**, Manikchand. Most of Siraj's army **did not fight**. Siraj was captured and killed | • **Mir Jafar** made Nawab (a puppet)<br>• Company got the **zamindari of the 24 Parganas**<br>• Huge sums paid to the Company and its officials<br>• The **French were eliminated from Bengal**<br>• The beginning of the **Drain of Wealth** 🔗 Block 2 |

### Mir Kasim and the Battle of Buxar (22 Oct 1764) 📌
- **Mir Kasim (1760):** ceded **Burdwan, Midnapore and Chittagong** to the Company. He was **able and independent**: shifted the capital to **Monghyr**, modernised the army, reorganised finances.
- **Conflict:** he **abolished internal duties for everyone** (so Indian traders were on a par with the Company), which angered the English.
- **Buxar:** **Major Hector Munro** defeated the **combined forces** of **Mir Kasim + Shuja-ud-Daulah (Nawab of Awadh) + Shah Alam II (Mughal Emperor)**.

### Treaty of Allahabad (Aug 1765), signed by Clive 📌
| With Shah Alam II (Mughal) | With Shuja-ud-Daulah (Awadh) |
|---|---|
| Emperor granted the **Diwani** (right to collect revenue) of **Bengal, Bihar and Orissa** to the Company | Awadh **returned** to Shuja, except **Kora and Allahabad** |
| Company to pay the emperor **₹26 lakh a year** | Shuja pays **₹50 lakh** as war indemnity |
| **Kora and Allahabad** ceded to the emperor | Company to **defend Awadh's frontier** at Awadh's cost: an early form of the **buffer state** idea |

### Dual Government in Bengal (1765–72) 📌
- Introduced by **Robert Clive**.
- **Company:** **Diwani** (revenue). **Nawab:** **Nizamat** (police, judicial administration). Result: **power without responsibility** for the Company; **responsibility without power** for the Nawab.
- Deputy Diwans: **Muhammad Reza Khan** (Bengal), **Raja Shitab Rai** (Bihar).
- Result: oppression and the **Great Bengal Famine of 1770**, in which about **one-third of Bengal's population** died.
- **Abolished by Warren Hastings in 1772**.

### Plassey vs Buxar: Why Buxar was more decisive 📌
| Plassey (1757) | Buxar (1764) |
|---|---|
| Won by **treachery** more than by fighting | A **real military victory** |
| Defeated **one nawab** | Defeated **three powers** together, **including the Mughal emperor** |
| Gave the English **political influence** in Bengal | Gave them the **legal right (Diwani)** to Bengal's revenue: **legitimacy** |
| | Made the English a **pan-North-Indian power**. Awadh became dependent |

🧠 **"Plassey = Political (Puppet), Buxar = Big & Binding (Diwani)"**

## 1.5 Policies of Expansion 📌📌

### Ring Fence Policy (Warren Hastings, 1772–85)
- Defend the **neighbours' frontiers** in order to safeguard the Company's own territories, i.e., create **buffer zones** (e.g., Awadh against the Marathas and Afghans).
- The neighbouring states paid for the troops. It foreshadowed the Subsidiary Alliance.

### Subsidiary Alliance (Lord Wellesley, 1798–1805)
**Origin:** the idea was first used by the French under **Dupleix**. Wellesley made it a **system**.
**Terms:**
1. The Indian ruler **accepts a permanent British force** in his territory and **pays a subsidy** for it.
2. If he cannot pay, he **cedes territory**.
3. A **British Resident** stays at his court.
4. He **cannot employ any other Europeans** without British approval.
5. **No alliance or war** with other states without British consent (foreign policy surrendered).
6. In return, the Company **protects him** from external enemies and promises **not to interfere** in internal affairs. This promise was rarely kept.

**States that accepted (order):** 🧠 **"HMTAPBS"**: **H**yderabad (**1798**, the **first**) → **M**ysore (1799) → **T**anjore (1799) → **A**wadh (1801) → **P**eshwa (1802, Treaty of Bassein) → **B**honsle (1803) → **S**cindia (1804). Also Jodhpur, Jaipur, Macheri, Bundi, Bharatpur, among others.
**Effects:** rulers lost independence; armies were disbanded and soldiers became unemployed (and later joined the **Pindaris**); states went **bankrupt** paying subsidies; misgovernance followed, since the rulers had no accountability; the British gained an army paid for by Indians.

### Doctrine of Lapse (Lord Dalhousie, 1848–56)
- If a ruler of a **dependent state** died **without a natural heir**, the state would **"lapse"** to the British. An **adopted son** could inherit the ruler's **personal property**, but **not the kingdom**.
- **Annexed (order):** 🧠 **"S-J-S-B-U-J-N"**: **Satara (1848, the first)** → **Jaitpur and Sambalpur (1849)** → **Baghat (1850)** → **Udaipur** (Chhattisgarh, 1852) → **Jhansi (1853)** → **Nagpur (1854)**.
- ⚠️ **Awadh (1856)** was annexed on the grounds of **misgovernance** (Nawab **Wajid Ali Shah**), **not** under the Doctrine of Lapse.
- Dalhousie also **abolished titles and pensions** (e.g., Nana Saheb's pension as Baji Rao II's adopted son; the titular Nawab of Carnatic and Raja of Tanjore). These were major causes of the **1857 Revolt**. 🔗 Week 2

### Why the British succeeded in India (summary) 📌
| Factor | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Military superiority | Better arms (muskets, artillery), **discipline**, regular salaries, a **trained sepoy army** |
| Leadership | Clive, Hastings, Wellesley, Coote, Munro vs **divided** Indian leadership |
| Finance | **Bengal revenue** + **trade profits** + **credit from Indian bankers** (Jagat Seths) |
| Diplomacy | **Divide and rule**: allied with one Indian power against another (Nizam + Marathas vs Tipu) |
| Navy | Command of the seas |
| Political disunity in India | Post-Aurangzeb decline; rivalries among the Marathas, Mysore, Hyderabad and the Sikhs; no sense of "India" as a political unit |
| Industrial Revolution | Economic and technological strength of Britain |

# 📗 BLOCK 2 (Wed–Thu) – BRITISH ADMINISTRATION, ECONOMIC POLICIES & LAND REVENUE

## ⏱ Snapshot
- **Administration rested on 4 pillars: Civil Service, Army, Police, Judiciary.** The **rule of law** was introduced, but with **racial discrimination**.
- **3 stages of colonialism** (R.P. Dutt / Bipan Chandra): **Mercantilism (1757–1813)** → **Free trade / industrial capital (1813–1858)** → **Finance imperialism (1860 onwards)**.
- **Drain of Wealth**: **Dadabhai Naoroji** (*Poverty and Un-British Rule in India*, 1901); **R.C. Dutt** (*Economic History of India*).
- **Land revenue systems:** **Permanent Settlement** (Cornwallis 1793, about 19% of area), **Ryotwari** (Munro and Read, about 51%), **Mahalwari** (Holt Mackenzie 1822, about 30%).

## 2.1 Administrative Structure

### Civil Services 📌
| Year/Person | Development |
|---|---|
| **Lord Cornwallis** (1786–93) | **"Father of Civil Services in India"**. **Raised salaries** and banned private trade and gifts to curb corruption. **Reserved senior posts for Europeans** |
| **Lord Wellesley** | **Fort William College (1800)**, Calcutta, to train civil servants. The Court of Directors disapproved and replaced it in **1806** with **East India College, Haileybury** (England) |
| **Charter Act 1853** | **Open competition** introduced. The **Macaulay Committee (1854)** laid down the scheme |
| **1863** | **Satyendranath Tagore**, the **first Indian to qualify for the ICS** |
| **1876/1878–79 (Lytton)** | Maximum age **reduced from 21 to 19** (a handicap for Indians). **Statutory Civil Service (1878–79)**: 1/6th of posts by nomination of "Indians of good family"; **abolished in 1887** |
| **Aitchison Committee (1886)**, under Dufferin | Recommended dropping "covenanted/uncovenanted" terms; created **Imperial, Provincial and Subordinate** services; raised the age to 23 |
| **Montford Report (1918)** | Simultaneous exams in India and England; **1/3 of recruitment in India** (rising) |
| **Lee Commission (1924)** | Recommended a **Public Service Commission**, which led to the **Federal PSC (1926)**. 🔗 Polity: 1919 Act, 1935 Act |
| **Islington Commission (1912–15)** | Recommended 25% of higher posts for Indians |

### Army and Police
- **Army:** the sepoy army, with Indians **excluded from officer ranks**. After 1857, the **Peel Commission (1859)**: **increase the ratio of European to Indian troops** (about 1:2 in Bengal, 2:5 in Madras and Bombay); the "**divide and counterpoise**" policy (mix castes and regions in regiments); the **"martial races"** theory (recruit from Punjab, Gurkhas, Pathans, and so on); **artillery reserved for Europeans**.
- **Police:** **Cornwallis** relieved the zamindars of police duties. **Thanas** headed by **daroghas**, under the district **SP**. The **Police Act 1861** (still the basis of many state police laws). The **Police Commission (1902–03, Andrew Fraser, under Curzon)** recommended a **CID** at the Centre and in the provinces. 🔗 Polity / Governance: police reforms, *Prakash Singh (2006)*.

### Judicial System 📌
| Person | Reforms |
|---|---|
| **Warren Hastings (1772)** | **Diwani Adalats** (civil) and **Faujdari Adalats** (criminal) in districts. Appeals to the **Sadar Diwani Adalat** and **Sadar Nizamat Adalat** at Calcutta. Hindu law (from the shastras) and Muslim law (sharia) applied to personal matters. **Supreme Court at Calcutta (1774)** under the **Regulating Act** |
| **Cornwallis (1793), the "Cornwallis Code"** | **Separated revenue and judicial administration**: the **Collector lost judicial powers**. Created district judges and **circuit courts**. Established the **"sovereignty of law"**: government officials were answerable to courts for official acts |
| **William Bentinck (1828–35)** | Abolished the provincial courts of appeal and circuit (Cornwallis's), whose work went to commissioners. **Persian replaced by vernaculars** in lower courts; **English** in higher courts. **Law Commission** under **Macaulay** (Charter Act 1833) |
| **1860s** | **IPC (1860)**, drafted by Macaulay; **CrPC (1861)**; **Civil Procedure Code (1859)**. **High Courts Act (1861)**: HCs at **Calcutta, Bombay, Madras (1862)**, replacing the Sadar Adalats and the SC |
| **Ilbert Bill (1883), under Ripon** | Proposed to let **Indian judges try Europeans**. The **European backlash** forced a compromise: Europeans could demand a **jury of at least 50% Europeans**. It **exposed racial arrogance** and spurred nationalism |

**Positive and negative:** the rule of law, codified laws and equality before law **in principle**. But justice was **costly and slow**, Europeans had **special privileges**, and **racial discrimination** persisted.
📰 **Link:** the **BNS, BNSS and BSA (2023, in force 1 July 2024)** replaced Macaulay's IPC, the CrPC and the Evidence Act (1872). This is the "decolonisation of criminal law".

## 2.2 Economic Impact of British Rule 📌📌

### Three stages of colonialism
| Stage | Period | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Mercantilism (Merchant capital)** | **1757–1813** | **Monopoly trade** and **direct appropriation** of revenue (plunder) after Plassey. Buy Indian goods with Indian revenue ("**investment**") and sell them in Europe. **No large import of British goods** yet |
| **2. Free Trade (Industrial capital)** | **1813–1858** | The Charter Act 1813 ended the monopoly. India became a **market for British manufactures** (cotton textiles) and a **supplier of raw materials** (cotton, indigo, jute). **One-way free trade**: British goods entered India almost duty-free, while Indian goods faced **high tariffs** in Britain. This led to **deindustrialisation** |
| **3. Finance Imperialism (Finance capital)** | **1860 onwards** | **British capital invested** in **railways**, loans to the Govt of India, tea plantations, coal mines, jute mills, banking, shipping. **Guaranteed returns** (e.g., **5% for railways**) were paid by Indian taxpayers |

### Deindustrialisation 📌
**Causes:**
1. **Loss of royal patronage**: Indian courts disappeared with annexation.
2. **Competition from machine-made goods** (Lancashire cotton).
3. **One-way free trade** and discriminatory tariffs.
4. **Railways** opened the interior to British goods.
5. **Company oppression** of artisans (forced low prices, the weavers of Bengal).
6. **New patterns of demand**: an English-educated elite with Western tastes.

**Effects:** artisans moved to agriculture (**ruralisation** and **overburdening of land**), decline of towns (**Dhaka, Murshidabad, Surat**), unemployment, loss of the export economy. ⚠️ India became an **exporter of raw cotton and an importer of cloth**.

### Drain of Wealth 📌📌
- **Theorists:** **Dadabhai Naoroji**, the "**Grand Old Man of India**" (paper "*England's Debt to India*" **1867**; book ***Poverty and Un-British Rule in India*, 1901**); **R.C. Dutt** (***The Economic History of India***, 1901–03); **M.G. Ranade**; G.V. Joshi, G. Subramania Iyer, **G.K. Gokhale**.
- **Meaning:** the part of India's national product that went to Britain **without any equivalent economic, commercial or material return**.
- **Channels (components):**
  1. **Home Charges**: pensions and salaries of British officials, interest on public debt held in Britain, expenses of the **India Office** in London, expenses of wars fought outside India (Afghan, Burmese, Abyssinian)
  2. Salaries and savings remitted by British officials in India
  3. **Profits on foreign investment** (railways, plantations, mines)
  4. **Interest on loans**
  5. **Council Bills** (a mechanism for transferring money to London)
  6. Payments for shipping, banking and insurance services
- **Effects:** **capital starvation** (so no industrialisation), **poverty**, **famines**, **unemployment**, and a high tax burden.
- **Significance:** the **economic critique of colonialism** demolished the myth of the "benevolent Raj" and gave **nationalism an economic basis**. 🔗 Week 2: Moderates.

### Commercialisation of Agriculture
- Peasants grew **cash crops** (indigo, cotton, jute, opium, tea, sugarcane) to pay **cash revenue**, often **forced** (e.g., **indigo planters**, which led to the **Indigo Revolt of 1859–60**, Week 2).
- **Effects:** dependence on **moneylenders and traders**; **food insecurity**; vulnerability to world prices (the **US Civil War cotton boom and bust** of the 1860s, which led to the **Deccan Riots of 1875**); recurring **famines**.
- **Famine Commissions:** **Strachey (1880)**, which led to the Famine Code (1883); **Lyall (1898)**; **MacDonnell (1901)**. The **Bengal Famine of 1943** (about 3 million deaths) is a separate later topic.

### Infrastructure (with motives)
| Infrastructure | Key facts | Real motive |
|---|---|---|
| **Railways** | **First train: 16 April 1853, Bombay–Thane (34 km)**, under **Dalhousie** ("Father of Indian Railways"). Built under the **guarantee system** (5% assured returns) | Carry raw materials to the ports and British goods inland; move troops |
| **Telegraph** | **1853**, Calcutta–Agra line (Dalhousie); **O'Shaughnessy** | Administrative and military control (it proved useful to the British in **1857**) |
| **Postal system** | **Post Office Act 1854**: uniform postage rates (half-anna postcards came later) | Administrative unification |
| **Irrigation** | Upper Ganga Canal (1854); Cauvery anicut repairs (Arthur Cotton) | Revenue and cash crops |

⚠️ **Unintended effect:** railways, the post and the telegraph **unified India** and **helped nationalism** spread (communication among nationalists, newspapers, all-India organisations). 🔗 Week 2

## 2.3 Land Revenue Systems 📌📌📌 (very high frequency)

### Background
- **1765:** the Diwani gave the Company revenue rights.
- **Warren Hastings: Izaradari / Farming system (1772)**: revenue collection auctioned to the **highest bidder** for **5 years** (quinquennial), changed to **annual** settlements in 1777. **Failure:** over-bidding, ruin of peasants.
- **John Shore** planned the Permanent Settlement; **Cornwallis** implemented it.

### The three systems: master comparison
| Feature | **Permanent Settlement (Zamindari)** | **Ryotwari** | **Mahalwari** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Introduced by | **Lord Cornwallis** (with **John Shore**), **22 March 1793**. The **Decennial (10-year) Settlement of 1789–90** was made **permanent** | **Thomas Munro** and **Captain Alexander Read**. Read experimented in **Baramahal (1792)**; Munro made it general in **Madras (1820)** as Governor. **Bombay**: **Mountstuart Elphinstone**, with Wingate and Goldsmid surveys (1830s) | **Holt Mackenzie (1822)**, **Regulation VII of 1822**. Revised under **Bentinck** by **R.M. Bird** and **James Thomason** (**Regulation IX of 1833**). **R.M. Bird** is called the "**father of land settlement in North India**" |
| Regions | **Bengal, Bihar, Orissa**, the **Banaras division** (UP), **northern Carnatic** | **Madras, Bombay**, parts of **Assam**, **Coorg** | **North-Western Provinces** (Ganga valley, UP), **Punjab**, parts of **Central India** |
| % of British Indian area | About **19%** | About **51%** (the **largest**) | About **30%** |
| Settlement made with | **Zamindars** (as owners) | **Ryots** (cultivators) directly | The **village community (mahal)**. The **lambardar** (headman) collected |
| Ownership | **Zamindar** recognised as **owner** of the land. Ryots became **tenants** | **Ryot** recognised as **owner** (as long as revenue was paid) | Ownership with the **village community / individual proprietors** jointly |
| Revenue rate | Fixed at **10/11th** (about **89%**) of the rental for the state; **1/11th** for the zamindar | **1/2 (dry) to 3/5 (wet)** of the produce **initially**, later reduced (e.g., about 1/3 under later settlements) | Started at about **80%** (1822), reduced to **66%** (Bird–Thomason), later **50%** (**Saharanpur Rules, 1855**, under Dalhousie) |
| Fixed or revised | **Fixed permanently** (never revised) | **Periodically revised** (20–30 years) | **Periodically revised** |
| Key rule | **"Sunset Law"**: if revenue was not paid **by sunset** on the due date, the estate was **auctioned** | State acted as a **"super-landlord"** | **Joint and several** responsibility of the village |
| Problems | • State lost all **future increases** in revenue<br>• Peasants **rack-rented** (no protection until the Bengal Rent Act 1859/1885)<br>• **Absentee landlordism**<br>• **Subinfeudation** (layers of intermediaries, e.g., **patnidars**)<br>• Old zamindars **ruined** in early decades by the sunset law; **new merchant-moneylender zamindars** emerged | • **Over-assessment** and revenue fixed **irrespective of harvest**<br>• Peasants fell to **moneylenders**, then land was **alienated**<br>• Officials were often **corrupt and coercive** (Madras Torture Commission, 1855)<br>• Led to the **Deccan Riots (1875)**, then the **Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act (1879)** | • **Over-assessment** (the high early rates)<br>• Village elites (lambardars) dominated<br>• Land transfers to moneylenders<br>• **Discontent in the NWP and Awadh**, a background to **1857** |
| Motives (British) | **Assured, stable revenue**; a **loyal class** of zamindars (political support); less administrative cost; hoped zamindars would **invest in land** | **Higher revenue** by removing intermediaries; Munro's belief in the **"Indian tradition"** of ryot cultivation | Fit the **village-community** tradition of North India |

🧠 **Memory hooks:**
- **"C-Z-B 1793"**: **C**ornwallis, **Z**amindari, **B**engal, 1793
- **"M-R-M"**: **M**unro, **R**yotwari, **M**adras
- **"H-M-M"**: **H**olt **M**ackenzie, **M**ahalwari
- **Area: Ryotwari > Mahalwari > Permanent** (**51 > 30 > 19**), "**R-M-P**: **R**ich **M**en **P**ay"

### Overall impact of the land revenue policies 📌
1. **Land became a marketable commodity**: it could be bought, sold and mortgaged, which led to **land alienation**.
2. Rise of **moneylenders and absentee landlords**; peasants became **tenants-at-will** and **landless labourers**.
3. **Stagnation of agriculture**: no investment, falling productivity.
4. **Commercialisation and famines** (see above).
5. **Social tension and peasant movements**: Indigo (1859–60), Pabna (1873–76), Deccan (1875). 🔗 Week 2
6. **Post-1947 legacy**: **zamindari abolition** and **land reforms** (1950s), protected by the **1st Amendment (Arts 31A, 31B, the Ninth Schedule)**. 🔗 Polity Day 5 ✔️

# 📗 BLOCK 3 (Fri–Sat) – SOCIO-RELIGIOUS REFORM MOVEMENTS

## ⏱ Snapshot
- **19th-century "Indian Renaissance"**: a response to the **colonial encounter** (Western education, Christian missionaries, Orientalist scholarship, the printing press).
- **Two broad strands:**
  - **Reformist**: modernise religion with reason and conscience (Brahmo Samaj, Prarthana Samaj, Aligarh).
  - **Revivalist**: return to a "pure" original tradition (Arya Samaj, Deoband).
- Bengal was first, followed by Western India, then South India, Punjab and the North. **Lower-caste movements** (Phule, Narayana Guru, Ayyankali, Periyar) challenged the **caste hierarchy itself**.
- The main targets: **sati, child marriage, the ban on widow remarriage, purdah, caste and untouchability, idolatry, and priestly dominance**.

## 3.1 Why did reform happen? (Causes) 📌
1. **Western education and rationalism**: new ideas of liberty, equality, humanism, science.
2. **Christian missionaries**: their criticism of Hindu practices provoked **defensive reform**. Serampore (Carey, Marshman, Ward) also ran printing and schools.
3. **Orientalist scholarship**: William Jones and Max Müller recovered a "glorious past", which built **self-confidence**.
4. **Printing press and newspapers**: spread of ideas.
5. **Rise of a new middle class** in the presidency towns of **Calcutta, Bombay and Madras**. ⚠️ This is why Bengal and Bombay came first.
6. **Colonial rule itself**: the humiliation of subjugation prompted introspection ("why were we conquered?").

### Two intellectual criteria of the reformers
- **Rationalism**: test practices against reason (Ram Mohan Roy rejected sati and idolatry as irrational).
- **Religious universalism**: all religions share a common truth (Ram Mohan Roy, Keshub, Vivekananda).

### Reformist vs Revivalist
| | Reformist | Revivalist |
|---|---|---|
| Approach | Reform religion and society in light of **reason and Western liberal thought** | Return to the **pure, ancient tradition**, seen as already superior |
| Attitude to the West | Open, borrowing | Critical, self-assertive |
| Examples (Hindu) | Brahmo Samaj, Prarthana Samaj, Young Bengal, Aligarh (Muslim) | **Arya Samaj** ("Back to the Vedas"), Ramakrishna/Vivekananda (partly), Theosophists, Deoband (Muslim) |
| ⚠️ Overlap | Both relied on **scripture** for legitimacy: Ram Mohan cited the Upanishads, Vidyasagar the **Parashara Samhita** (to justify widow remarriage) | Revivalists also attacked caste, child marriage and idolatry |

## 3.2 Bengal 📌📌

### Raja Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833): "Father of the Indian Renaissance" / "Maker of Modern India"
| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| Organisations | **Atmiya Sabha (1814/1815)** → **Brahmo Sabha (20 Aug 1828)**, later renamed the **Brahmo Samaj** |
| Beliefs | **Monotheism**, **opposed idolatry** and ritualism; drew on the **Upanishads**; **religious universalism** |

### Young Bengal Movement 📌
- **Henry Louis Vivian Derozio** (1809–31), a teacher at the **Hindu College**. A **radical rationalist** influenced by the **French Revolution**.
- Encouraged **free thinking**, questioning authority and religious orthodoxy.
- **Removed from Hindu College (1831)** and **died the same year** of cholera, aged 22.
- **Academic Association**; the newspaper ***East Indian***.
- **Limitation:** remained limited to a small group; called "too radical"; little mass base.

### Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar (1820–91) 📌
- **Principal of Sanskrit College**, which he **opened to non-Brahmins**; he introduced Western thought there.
- **Widow remarriage:** cited the **Parashara Samhita** (scriptural sanction). This led to the **Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act 1856** (**Act XV**, passed under **Lord Canning**; the drafting began under **Dalhousie**). The **first lawful Hindu widow remarriage** was held in Calcutta on **7 Dec 1856**.
- Campaigned against **child marriage** and **polygamy**.
- **Education:** **Bethune School (1849)** for girls (**J.E.D. Bethune**; Vidyasagar was its secretary); set up about **35 girls' schools**; ***Barnaparichay*** (the Bengali primer).
- His title "**Vidyasagar**" means "**ocean of learning**".

### Other Bengal names
- **Dharma Sabha (1830)**, **Radhakanta Deb**: **orthodox**, opposed sati abolition and appealed to the Privy Council.
- **Ramakrishna Paramahamsa and Vivekananda**: see 3.5.

# 🧩 ANSWER FRAMEWORKS: Critical & Analytical Questions (Modern History)

## Block 1
**QH1.1 Military genius vs Indian fragmentation**
- *Fragmentation:* post-Aurangzeb decline; regional powers (Marathas, Mysore, Hyderabad, Sikhs, Awadh, Bengal) were rivals; the English **allied with one against another** (Nizam and Marathas vs Tipu in 1790–92; Maratha internal feuds such as Raghoba and later the Peshwa vs Holkar); traitors at Plassey; bankers (the Jagat Seths) backed the Company.
- *But British strengths mattered:* disciplined infantry, artillery, regular pay; naval control; **Bengal's revenue** as a financial base; leaders like Clive, Wellesley, Coote; diplomacy (the Subsidiary Alliance); the Company's ability to raise **credit**.
- *Counter-evidence:* Indian rulers **modernised** too (Tipu's rockets, Ranjit Singh's army, Mahadji Sindhia's French-trained troops). Yet they lost, owing to **organisation, finance and unity**, not just weapons.
- *Conclusion:* a **combination**. Indian disunity provided the opportunity; British institutional strengths (finance, organisation, sea power) converted it into empire.

**QH1.2 Trade privileges → conflict:** The 1717 farman gave duty-free trade to the **Company**. Its servants **misused dastaks for private trade**, causing a revenue loss to the Nawab and unfair competition for Indian merchants → Siraj's resistance (plus fortification and the asylum issue) → Plassey. Mir Kasim's abolition of all duties → Buxar. *Lesson:* the Company's **commercial interest needed political control**: "trade followed the flag and the flag followed trade".

**QH1.3 Subsidiary Alliance consequences**
- *Political:* rulers lost **sovereignty** (foreign policy, the Resident's interference) and became puppets. Accountability to their subjects disappeared, so **misgovernance** followed, which later justified **annexation** (Awadh).
- *Economic:* the **subsidy** burden led to bankruptcy, then cession of territory (the Nizam ceded the Ceded Districts in 1800; Awadh ceded half its territory in 1801) and oppressive taxation of peasants.
- *Military:* native armies were disbanded, causing **unemployment** of soldiers (who became Pindaris); the British got a **large army paid by Indians**, stationed at strategic points.
- *Conclusion:* "**conquest without war**", the most cost-effective tool of empire.

**QH1.4 Tipu vs Ranjit Singh**
- **Tipu:** saw the British as an existential threat. Tried **foreign alliances** (France, the Ottomans); modernised; but was **isolated** (the Nizam and Marathas joined the British). Fought 4 wars and died in 1799.
- **Ranjit Singh:** realist. The **Treaty of Amritsar (1809)** conceded the cis-Sutlej region and avoided conflict while he **consolidated Punjab** and modernised his army (European officers). The Sikh state fell **after** his death (1839), owing to internal anarchy (1845–49).
- *Lesson:* **pragmatic accommodation** delayed conquest; **confrontation without allies** hastened it. Neither could solve the problem of **succession and unity**.

**QH1.5 Institutional vs individual:** Dupleix's brilliance (puppet rulers, the use of sepoys, the subsidiary idea) could not overcome a **state-controlled, under-funded company**, a weak navy, a home government that preferred European wars, and his own recall. The English had **institutional advantages**: a private joint-stock company, the Bank of England's credit, naval supremacy, Bengal's revenue. *Lesson:* **structure beats individual genius over the long run**.

**QH1.6 Dual Government and today:** Dual Government meant the Company had **power without responsibility**. The Indian Constitution's **parliamentary system** ensures **collective responsibility** of the Council of Ministers to the Lok Sabha (**Art 75(3)**). The **nominal (President) vs real (PM)** executive is reconciled through **Art 74** (aid and advice): power and accountability rest in the same hands.

## Block 2
**QH2.1 Unified administratively, impoverished economically**
- *Unified:* a common administration, the civil service, codified laws (IPC, CrPC), the rule of law, railways, post, telegraph, a common language of administration (English), census and survey (the Survey of India); a pan-Indian consciousness.
- *Impoverished:* the drain of wealth, deindustrialisation, the land revenue burden, commercialisation, famines (about 30 million+ deaths in the late 19th century, per estimates), low growth (per capita income growth was nearly zero, 1900–1947), illiteracy (about 84% in 1947), life expectancy around 32.
- *Nuance:* the "unification" was meant for **control**, and **unintentionally** helped nationalism; the administration was racially exclusive.
- *Conclusion:* both statements are true. **Unity was a by-product; poverty was structural.**

**QH2.2 Three land systems compared**
- **Beneficiaries:** zamindars (Permanent Settlement), the state (Ryotwari and Mahalwari, through periodic revision), moneylenders (all three).
- **Losers:** peasants in all three. Under the Permanent Settlement they were tenants-at-will; under Ryotwari, over-assessed; under Mahalwari, village elites dominated them.
- **British gains:** stable revenue (PS); maximum revenue (Ryotwari); political collaborators (the zamindars).
- **Why not extended after 1793:** the state lost all **future increases** in revenue as land values and prices rose (the "loss of unearned increment"); there was no traditional zamindar class in many regions (Madras, Bombay); Munro's and Utilitarian (Ricardo's rent theory) ideas favoured **direct settlement with periodic revision**.

**QH2.3 Drain Theory**
- *Components:* Home Charges, official remittances, profits on investment, interest, Council Bills.
- *Political impact:* (1) it shifted the critique from "bad officials" to the **system** of colonialism itself; (2) it gave an **economic, secular** basis to nationalism; (3) it became the core of **Moderate** politics (petitions and INC resolutions); (4) it inspired **Swadeshi** (1905) and later Gandhian economics; (5) it influenced the Congress Karachi Resolution (1931).
- *Limitation:* the Moderates still hoped British justice would correct it.

**QH2.4 Railways: gift or tool?**
- *Colonial motive:* carry raw materials to the ports and finished goods inland; move **troops** quickly (post-1857); the guarantee system meant profits for British investors at Indian expense; freight rates discriminated in favour of port traffic; little forward linkage to Indian industry (rails and locomotives were imported).
- *Nationalist outcome:* national integration; spread of ideas, newspapers and political workers; famine relief (later); labour movements (railway strikes).
- *Conclusion:* a **tool of exploitation** that became an **unintended agent of unification**.

**QH2.5 Cornwallis Code and Art 50:** In 1793 the Collector (the revenue officer) **lost his judicial powers** to district judges, which meant **separation of revenue/executive and judicial functions**. This idea matured into **Art 50 (DPSP)**: "the State shall take steps to separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services". It was implemented by **CrPC 1973** (judicial magistrates vs executive magistrates), and continued in **BNSS 2023**.

**QH2.6 Deindustrialisation and disguised unemployment:** The colonial destruction of crafts **pushed artisans into agriculture** (the share of the workforce in agriculture rose in the late 19th century). After 1947, slow industrialisation kept the workforce on the land. Today about **45% of the workforce** is in agriculture but it produces only about **18%** of GVA, meaning low marginal productivity and **disguised unemployment**. *Way forward:* labour-intensive manufacturing (PLI, MSMEs, textiles), skilling, rural non-farm employment. 🔗 Economy, Week 5.

**QH2.7 Ilbert Bill and organised agitation:** The Europeans' **organised, well-funded agitation** (the Defence Association) **forced the government to back down**. Indians learnt that (1) the Raj was **racially discriminatory** and (2) **organised political pressure works**. Together with the **Indian Association (1876)**'s campaigns (civil service age, the VPA, the Arms Act) and the **National Conference (1883)**, this paved the way to the **INC (1885)**. 🔗 Week 2.

## Block 3
**QH3.1 Reformist intent, revivalist method**
- *Reformist intent:* the goals were modern: rationalism, women's rights, anti-caste, education.
- *Revivalist method:* legitimised through scripture (Ram Mohan: the Upanishads; Vidyasagar: the Parashara Samhita; Dayanand: the Vedas; Sir Syed: the Quran interpreted rationally). They did this to win **acceptance in an orthodox society** and to counter missionary attacks.
- *Exceptions:* Derozio and Young Bengal (purely rationalist); **Phule and Periyar** (rejected scriptural authority altogether).
- *Consequence:* it kept religion central to public life and contributed to **religious identity politics** later.
- *Conclusion:* a **strategic hybrid**, effective in its time but with long-term costs.

**QH3.2 Upper-caste vs lower-caste reformers**
- *Upper-caste:* the focus was on **women's issues within upper-caste households** (sati, widow remarriage, child marriage), **monotheism**, education. Caste was criticised but **reform from within**.
- *Lower-caste:* caste **was** the system of oppression (Phule's *Gulamgiri*: Brahmanism as slavery); **education, dignity, access** (roads, temples, schools: Ayyankali, Vaikom); **counter-institutions** (Satyashodhak, SNDP, consecrating their own temples).
- *Why caste:* their **lived experience** (untouchability, exclusion from education and public spaces) and the economic subordination tied to caste. Reforming religion without abolishing caste meant nothing to them.
- *Legacy:* Ambedkar, the Dravidian movement, the constitutional provisions (Arts 15, 17, 46).

**QH3.3 Reform → INC?**
- *Yes:* they created an **educated, critical middle class**; associations and a **public sphere**; leaders (Ranade, Gokhale, Besant, Lajpat Rai) moved from social to political work; **pride in India's heritage**; the press.
- *But:* the INC also grew from **economic critique** (drain), **political associations** (British Indian Association 1851, Indian Association 1876, Poona Sarvajanik Sabha 1870, Madras Mahajan Sabha 1884), **administrative unification**, and **racial discrimination** (the Ilbert Bill).
- *Conclusion:* a **necessary but not sufficient** condition.

**QH3.4 Press and reform:** Ram Mohan Roy: *Sambad Kaumudi*, *Mirat-ul-Akhbar*. Debendranath: *Tattvabodhini Patrika*. Sir Syed: *Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq*. Parsis: *Rast Goftar*. Veeresalingam: *Viveka Vardhini*. Vivekananda: *Prabuddha Bharata*, *Udbodhan*. Periyar: *Kudi Arasu*. Derozio: *East Indian*. The press spread **debate**, built **communities of readers**, and turned local reform into **regional and national** conversations.

**QH3.5 "About women, without women"**
- Most legislative reforms were **led by men** (Ram Mohan, Vidyasagar, Malabari). Women were the "**objects**" of reform.
- But women **spoke too**:
  - **Pandita Ramabai** (critique of high-caste patriarchy, Sharada Sadan)
  - **Tarabai Shinde** (*Stri Purush Tulana*, 1882)
  - **Savitribai Phule** (teaching, poetry)
  - **Rassundari Devi** (*Amar Jiban*, 1876)
  - **Rukhmabai** (refused child-marriage consummation, which led to the age-of-consent debate; later one of India's first women doctors)
  - **Anandibai Joshi** (an early woman doctor)
- *Conclusion:* the statement is **largely true of the legislative reforms**, but women's agency was present, and it was marginalised in historical memory.

**QH3.6 Vaikom and the Constitution:**
- **Art 15(2)**: no restriction on access to roads, wells, tanks, public places on grounds of caste. This is **directly Vaikom's demand**.
- **Art 17**: abolition of untouchability.
- **Art 25(2)(b)**: the State may **throw open Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus**. This is **the Temple Entry demand** (Guruvayur, the 1936 Proclamation).

The Constitution **constitutionalised the demands** of these movements.

**QH3.7 Shuddhi/Tabligh and revivalism:** The Arya Samaj's **Shuddhi** (reconversion) and the Muslim **Tabligh/Tanzim** responses (1920s), after the Khilafat movement collapsed, drew on revivalist ideas of a "pure" community and **numbers as power** (the census categories). This led to communal riots (1920s). *Lesson:* reform that defines identity **against an "other"** can harden boundaries. Reform grounded in **constitutional values** (equality, dignity, fraternity) is more inclusive.
