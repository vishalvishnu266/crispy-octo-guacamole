# 📘 DAY 1 (Mon) – HISTORICAL BACKGROUND & MAKING OF THE CONSTITUTION

## ⏱ Snapshot (read this first, and again before sleeping)
- Constitutional development falls into **two phases**: **Company Rule (1773–1858)** and **Crown Rule (1858–1947)**.
- The overall trend runs **centralisation → association of Indians → representation → responsible government → independence**.
- The **1935 Act** is the "blueprint" of the Constitution. About **250 provisions** of the original Constitution were drawn from it, wholly or partly.
- **Constituent Assembly (CA):** set up under the **Cabinet Mission Plan, 1946**. It was **indirectly elected** by provincial assemblies, with princely-state members **nominated**. First met on **9 Dec 1946** and adopted the Constitution on **26 Nov 1949**. The Constitution came into force on **26 Jan 1950**.

---

## 1.1 Company Rule (1773–1858): the Acts at a glance

| Act | 📌 Key provisions (in order of exam importance) | One-line significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Regulating Act, 1773** | • Governor of Bengal became **Governor-General of Bengal** (1st: **Warren Hastings**), with an Executive Council of 4<br>• The Governors of Bombay and Madras were made **subordinate** to him<br>• **Supreme Court at Calcutta (1774)**: 1 Chief Justice (**Elijah Impey**) and 3 judges<br>• Company servants barred from private trade and from taking bribes<br>• The Court of Directors had to report on revenue, civil and military affairs to the British government | **First step** by the British Govt to **control and regulate** the Company. It recognised the Company's political and administrative functions and laid the foundation of central administration |
| **Amending Act, 1781** (Act of Settlement) | Exempted the GG-in-Council from SC jurisdiction for official acts; revenue matters excluded from SC jurisdiction | Corrected the defects of 1773 |
| **Pitt's India Act, 1784** | • Separated **commercial** functions (left with the Court of Directors) from **political** functions (given to a new **Board of Control**)<br>• This created the **"Double Government"** system<br>• Company territories were called **"British possessions in India"** for the first time | British Govt gained **supreme control** over Indian affairs |
| **Charter Act, 1793** | Renewed the Company's monopoly for 20 years | — |
| **Charter Act, 1813** | • **Ended the Company's trade monopoly** in India, **except** trade in **tea** and trade with **China**<br>• Asserted the sovereignty of the Crown<br>• Allowed **Christian missionaries** in<br>• **₹1 lakh a year for education** of Indians<br>• Empowered local governments to levy taxes | India opened up to all British merchants: the start of the "free trade" phase |
| **Charter Act, 1833** | • Governor-General of Bengal became **Governor-General of India** (1st: **Lord William Bentinck**), with all civil and military powers<br>• Created the **first Government of India** with authority over the whole of British India<br>• Bombay and Madras **lost their legislative powers**<br>• **Ended the Company's commercial activities**, leaving it a purely administrative body<br>• **Attempted** open competition for civil services. This was **negated** after the Court of Directors opposed it<br>• A Law Member (**Macaulay**) joined the council | **Final step towards centralisation** |
| **Charter Act, 1853** (last Charter Act) | • **Separated the legislative and executive functions** of the GG's Council: 6 new legislative members formed the **Central Legislative Council**, which worked like a **mini-Parliament**<br>• **Open competition for civil services introduced** (Macaulay Committee, 1854), now open to Indians as well<br>• Extended Company rule with **no fixed period**<br>• **Local representation** introduced: 4 of the 6 legislative members were appointed by the provincial governments of Madras, Bombay, Bengal and Agra | Beginning of **parliamentary-type** legislature |

## 1.2 Crown Rule (1858–1947)

| Act | 📌 Key provisions | One-line significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Government of India Act, 1858** ("Act for the Good Government of India") | • Passed after the **Revolt of 1857**; power transferred from the Company to the **Crown**<br>• GG became the **Viceroy** (1st: **Lord Canning**)<br>• **Ended Double Government** by abolishing the Board of Control and Court of Directors<br>• Created the **Secretary of State for India**, a member of the British Cabinet, with a **15-member Council of India**. The SoS was a corporate body that could sue and be sued | Mainly about improving the machinery of **administration**, not about changing the system of government |
| **Indian Councils Act, 1861** | • **Indians associated with law-making for the first time**: the Viceroy nominated some Indians as **non-official members**. In 1862 Canning nominated the **Raja of Benares, the Maharaja of Patiala and Sir Dinkar Rao**<br>• **Decentralisation**: legislative powers restored to Bombay and Madras<br>• New legislative councils for Bengal (1862), NWFP (1886) and Punjab (1897)<br>• Statutory recognition of the **portfolio system** (introduced by Canning in 1859)<br>• Viceroy empowered to issue **ordinances** in an emergency, valid for **6 months** | Start of **representative institutions** |
| **Indian Councils Act, 1892** | • More non-official members<br>• Councils could **discuss the budget** and **ask questions** of the executive<br>• **Limited and indirect** use of election: members were "nominated on recommendation", and the word "election" was not used | First indirect, limited **election** principle |
| **Indian Councils Act, 1909** (Morley–Minto Reforms) | • Central Legislative Council enlarged from **16 to 60** members<br>• **Official majority at the Centre**, **non-official majority in the provinces**<br>• Members could ask **supplementary questions** and move resolutions on the budget<br>• **First Indian in the Viceroy's Executive Council**: **Satyendra Prasad Sinha** (Law Member)<br>• **Communal representation**: **separate electorate for Muslims**. **Lord Minto** is called the **"Father of Communal Electorate"**<br>• Separate representation also for presidency corporations, chambers of commerce, universities and zamindars | Legalised **communalism** |
| **Government of India Act, 1919** (Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms) | • Preceded by the **August 1917 Declaration**, whose goal was "responsible government"<br>• Separated central and provincial subjects<br>• Provincial subjects split into **Transferred** (Governor + Ministers responsible to the legislature) and **Reserved** (Governor + Executive Council): this was **DYARCHY in the provinces**<br>• **Bicameralism and direct elections at the Centre**: Council of State (Upper) and Legislative Assembly (Lower)<br>• **3 of the 6 members** of the Viceroy's Executive Council (other than the Commander-in-Chief) to be Indians<br>• Communal representation extended to **Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians and Europeans**<br>• Limited franchise based on property, tax or education<br>• Office of **High Commissioner for India** in London<br>• Provided for a **Public Service Commission**, which led to the Central PSC in **1926**<br>• **Separated provincial budgets** from the central budget<br>• A **statutory commission after 10 years**, which became the Simon Commission | First step towards **responsible government** |
| **Simon Commission (Nov 1927)** | 7 British members and **no Indian**, so boycotted ("Simon Go Back"). The 1930 report recommended: abolishing dyarchy, responsible government in the provinces, a federation, and continuing communal electorates | Led to the **3 Round Table Conferences** → White Paper → Joint Select Committee → **1935 Act** |
| **Communal Award (Aug 1932)** | Ramsay MacDonald extended separate electorates to the **Depressed Classes**. Gandhi fasted in **Yerwada Jail**, which led to the **Poona Pact (Sep 1932)**: joint electorate kept, but with **reserved seats** for the Depressed Classes | 🔗 Modern History, Week 4 |
| **Government of India Act, 1935** | • Long and detailed: **321 Sections, 10 Schedules**<br>• Proposed an **All-India Federation** of provinces and princely states. It **never came into being** because the princely states did not join<br>• **Three lists**: Federal (**59**), Provincial (**54**), Concurrent (**36**). **Residuary powers with the Viceroy**<br>• **Abolished dyarchy in provinces** and introduced **Provincial Autonomy**<br>• Provided for **dyarchy at the Centre**, which **never came into operation**<br>• **Bicameralism in 6 of the 11 provinces**: Bengal, Bombay, Madras, Bihar, Assam, United Provinces<br>• Communal representation extended to **Depressed Classes, women and labour**<br>• **Abolished the Council of India**<br>• Franchise extended to about **10%** of the population<br>• Provided for the **RBI** (established 1935), Federal, Provincial and Joint PSCs, and a **Federal Court** (set up 1937) | **"Blueprint"** of the Constitution. Nehru called it "a machine with strong brakes but no engine" and "a charter of slavery" |
| **Indian Independence Act, 1947** | • Based on the **Mountbatten Plan (3 June 1947)**, after **Attlee's announcement of 20 Feb 1947**<br>• India became independent and sovereign from **15 Aug 1947**, partitioned into **two dominions** (India and Pakistan), each **free to leave the Commonwealth**<br>• **Abolished the office of Viceroy**. Each dominion got a Governor-General, appointed by the King on the advice of the dominion cabinet<br>• Constituent Assemblies empowered to **frame any constitution** and to **repeal any British Act, including this Act itself**<br>• **CA also became the Central Legislature**<br>• **Abolished the office of Secretary of State**<br>• **Lapse of British paramountcy** over princely states, which were free to join India or Pakistan or stay independent<br>• The King dropped the title "Emperor of India"<br>• GG and provincial Governors became **constitutional (nominal) heads** | End of British rule. **Lord Mountbatten** became the 1st GG of free India. **C. Rajagopalachari** was the first and last **Indian** GG |

### 🧠 Memory hooks
- **"R-P-C-C-C"** for Company Acts: **R**egulating (1773), **P**itt's (1784), **C**harter 1813 (trade opened), **C**harter 1833 (Centralised, GG of India), **C**harter 1853 (Civil services competition, Council for legislation).
- **"1909 = Muslims, 1919 = Sikhs + others, 1932/35 = Depressed classes + women + labour"**: the communal electorate widened step by step.
- **Dyarchy**: provinces in **1919**, Centre in **1935** (never implemented). The 1935 Act **abolished** provincial dyarchy.
- **Firsts:** 1st GG of Bengal = Hastings (1773) · 1st GG of India = Bentinck (1833) · 1st Viceroy = Canning (1858) · 1st Indian on the Viceroy's Executive Council = S.P. Sinha (1909).

### ⚠️ UPSC traps
| Trap statement | Truth |
|---|---|
| "The Charter Act 1833 introduced open competition for civil services" | It only **attempted** to. It was actually introduced by the **1853** Act |
| "The 1935 Act established the Federation of India" | It **provided for** one, but the federation **never came into existence** |
| "The 1919 Act introduced dyarchy at the Centre" | It introduced dyarchy in the **provinces**. Central dyarchy was in the **1935** Act |
| "The 1892 Act introduced direct elections" | It used only **indirect** election (nomination on recommendation). **Direct** elections came at the Centre in **1919** |
| "The 1909 Act gave Indians majority in the Central Legislative Council" | It kept an **official majority at the Centre**. The non-official majority was only in the **provinces** |

## 1.3 Making of the Constitution

### A. Demand for a Constituent Assembly (chronology)
| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| **1934** | **M.N. Roy** first put forward the idea of a Constituent Assembly |
| **1935** | **INC officially** demanded a CA |
| **1938** | **Nehru**: the Constitution of free India must be framed by a CA elected on adult franchise without outside interference |
| **1940** | **August Offer**: the British accepted the demand in principle |
| **1942** | **Cripps Mission**: proposed a CA after the war. Rejected by the Muslim League, which wanted two separate assemblies |
| **1946** | **Cabinet Mission Plan**: the CA was formed under this scheme |

### B. Composition (Cabinet Mission scheme) 📌
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Total strength | **389**: **296** from British India (**292** from Governors' provinces + **4** from Chief Commissioners' provinces) and **93** from princely states |
| Basis | About **1 seat per 10 lakh** population |
| Communities | British India seats were divided among **Muslims, Sikhs and General** in proportion to population |
| Method | Provincial members **indirectly elected** by members of provincial legislative assemblies through **proportional representation by single transferable vote** |
| Princely states | Representatives **nominated** by the rulers |
| Nature | **Partly elected, partly nominated**. **Not** directly elected on adult franchise |
| July–Aug 1946 elections | **INC 208**, **Muslim League 73**, small groups/independents **15**. The 93 princely-state seats were **not filled** because the states stayed away |

### C. Working of the Assembly (key dates) 📌
| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **9 Dec 1946** | **First meeting**. 211 members attended and the **Muslim League boycotted**. **Dr Sachchidananda Sinha** (the oldest member) became **temporary President**, following the French practice |
| **11 Dec 1946** | **Dr Rajendra Prasad** elected **President**. **H.C. Mukherjee** and **V.T. Krishnamachari** became Vice-Presidents. **Sir B.N. Rau** was the **Constitutional Adviser** |
| **13 Dec 1946** | **Objectives Resolution** moved by **Nehru**, which later became the **Preamble** |
| **22 Jan 1947** | Objectives Resolution **adopted** |
| **22 July 1947** | **National Flag** adopted |
| **29 Aug 1947** | **Drafting Committee** set up under **Dr B.R. Ambedkar** |
| After the Independence Act | CA became a **fully sovereign body** and the **first Parliament of free India**. When it sat as a legislature, **G.V. Mavalankar** presided. After Partition its strength fell to **299** |
| **Feb 1948** | First draft published |
| **4 Nov 1948** | Ambedkar moved the draft (First Reading) |
| **May 1949** | Ratified India's **membership of the Commonwealth** |
| **26 Nov 1949** | Constitution **adopted** ("We… do hereby adopt, enact and give to ourselves…"). Now celebrated as **Constitution Day** (Samvidhan Divas, since 2015) |
| **24 Jan 1950** | Final session. **National Anthem** and **National Song** adopted. **Rajendra Prasad elected first President**. **284 members signed** the Constitution |
| **26 Jan 1950** | Constitution came into force (**Republic Day**) |
| 1950–52 | CA continued as the **Provisional Parliament** until the first elections (1951–52) |

**Facts:** 11 sessions · **2 years, 11 months, 18 days** · 165 days of sitting · draft considered for **114 days** · about **₹64 lakh** spent · Constitutions of about 60 countries consulted · **Elephant** was the CA's symbol.
**Original Constitution:** Preamble, **395 Articles, 22 Parts, 8 Schedules**. Calligraphy by **Prem Behari Narain Raizada** (English); decorated by **Nandalal Bose** and his students at Santiniketan; Hindi calligraphy by **Vasant Krishan Vaidya**.

### D. Provisions in force on 26 Nov 1949 (not 26 Jan 1950) 📌
**Citizenship, Elections, Provisional Parliament, Temporary and Transitional provisions, Short title** (Arts 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 60, 324, 366, 367, 379, 380, 388, 391, 392, 393).
🧠 Hook: **"CEPTS"**: **C**itizenship, **E**lections, **P**rovisional Parliament, **T**ransitional, **S**hort title.
**Why 26 Jan?** On **26 Jan 1930** the INC celebrated **Purna Swaraj Day** after the **Lahore Session (Dec 1929)** resolution.

### F. Criticisms of the CA (and the counter-points)
| Criticism | Counter-argument |
|---|---|
| **Not a representative body**: not directly elected on adult franchise | Elections were impractical in 1946. The members reflected almost every section of society; there were 15 women, and SCs and minorities were represented |
| **Not a sovereign body**: created by British proposals | After the 1947 Act it became fully sovereign. It drew its authority from "We, the People" |
| **Time-consuming** | It framed the world's longest constitution through democratic debate. The US took 4 months but for a far smaller text |
| **Dominated by Congress**: Granville Austin called it "a one-party body in an essentially one-party country" | Congress itself was a broad "umbrella". Non-Congress experts (Ambedkar, Alladi, N. Gopalaswamy) were included |
| **Lawyer-politician dominated**, so the language is legalistic | A legal document needs precision |
| **Hindu-dominated** | Seats reflected population. Minority rights (Arts 25–30) were robustly protected |

# 📘 DAY 2 (Tue) – SALIENT FEATURES & PREAMBLE

## ⏱ Snapshot
- India has the **lengthiest written constitution** in the world. As adopted: 395 Arts, 22 Parts, 8 Schedules. **Today: about 470 Articles, 25 Parts, 12 Schedules** (106 amendments as of 2025).
- It is **borrowed but not copied**: it adapts ideas from the 1935 Act and about 60 countries.
- It is **federal with a unitary bias**, a **parliamentary** form of government, and a **synthesis** of parliamentary sovereignty and judicial supremacy.
- The **Preamble**: amended **only once** (**42nd Amendment, 1976**, which added **Socialist, Secular, Integrity**). It **is part of the Constitution** (Kesavananda 1973). It is **non-justiciable**.

---

## 2.1 Salient Features: Quick Table

| # | Feature | Key points to remember |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Lengthiest written constitution** | Reasons: geographical size and diversity, the historical influence of the 1935 Act, a **single constitution for the Union and states**, and the dominance of legal experts in the CA |
| 2 | **Drawn from various sources** | See the sources table below. **Structural part** from the 1935 Act; **philosophical part** (FRs and DPSP) from the US and Ireland; **political part** (cabinet government, executive–legislature relations) from Britain |
| 3 | **Blend of rigidity and flexibility** | Some provisions need a simple majority, some a special majority, and some a special majority plus ratification by half the states (Art 368) |
| 4 | **Federal system with unitary bias** | The word **"Federation" is not used**. Art 1 says "**Union of States**". K.C. Wheare called it "**quasi-federal**"; Ivor Jennings "a federation with a strong centralising tendency"; Morris-Jones "**bargaining federalism**"; Granville Austin "**cooperative federalism**" |
| 5 | **Parliamentary form of government** | British (Westminster) model: nominal and real executives, majority-party rule, collective responsibility to the Lok Sabha, PM leadership, dissolution of the Lok Sabha. Adopted at both Centre and states |
| 6 | **Synthesis of parliamentary sovereignty and judicial supremacy** | Indian judicial review is narrower than the US's, because the US has "due process of law" and India has "procedure established by law" |
| 7 | **Integrated and independent judiciary** | A single system (SC → HC → subordinate courts) enforces both central and state laws, unlike the US dual system |
| 8 | **Fundamental Rights** (Part III) | Justiciable, not absolute, can be suspended in an Emergency (except Arts 20, 21) |
| 9 | **DPSP** (Part IV) | Ambedkar called them a "**novel feature**". Non-justiciable. They aim at a **welfare state** |
| 10 | **Fundamental Duties** (Part IVA) | Added in 1976 (42nd Amendment) on the **Swaran Singh Committee**'s recommendation. Originally 10, now **11** |
| 11 | **Secular state** | **Positive** secularism (equal respect for all religions), not the Western strict separation. "Secular" was added to the Preamble in 1976, but Arts 14, 15, 16, 25–30 and 44 already made the state secular |
| 12 | **Universal adult franchise** | Voting age lowered from **21 to 18** by the **61st Amendment Act, 1988** (in force 1989) |
| 13 | **Single citizenship** | From Britain. The US has dual citizenship |
| 14 | **Independent bodies** | ECI, CAG, UPSC, SPSC |
| 15 | **Emergency provisions** | National (Art 352), State/President's Rule (Art 356), Financial (Art 360). In an emergency the federal system turns unitary |
| 16 | **Three-tier government** | **73rd and 74th Amendments (1992)** added Panchayats (Part IX) and Municipalities (Part IXA) |
| 17 | **Co-operative societies** | **97th Amendment (2011)**: forming co-operatives became an FR under Art 19(1)(c); added Art 43B (DPSP) and Part IXB |

### 2.2 Sources of the Constitution 📌 (frequently asked)
| Source | Features borrowed |
|---|---|
| **Government of India Act, 1935** | Federal scheme, office of Governor, judiciary, Public Service Commissions, emergency provisions, administrative details |
| **Britain** | Parliamentary government, rule of law, legislative procedure, single citizenship, cabinet system, prerogative writs, parliamentary privileges, bicameralism |
| **USA** | Fundamental Rights, independence of judiciary, judicial review, impeachment of the President, removal of SC and HC judges, post of Vice-President |
| **Ireland** | DPSP, nomination of members to the Rajya Sabha, method of electing the President |
| **Canada** | Federation with a strong Centre, residuary powers with the Centre, Centre appoints state Governors, advisory jurisdiction of the SC |
| **Australia** | Concurrent List, freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse, **joint sitting** of the two Houses |
| **Weimar Germany** | Suspension of FRs during an Emergency |
| **USSR** | Fundamental Duties, ideal of **justice (social, economic and political)** in the Preamble |
| **France** | Republic, ideals of **liberty, equality and fraternity** in the Preamble |
| **South Africa** | Procedure for amending the Constitution, election of Rajya Sabha members |
| **Japan** | **Procedure established by law** |

🧠 **Memory hooks:**
- **US = "Rights + Judges + Impeach + VP"** · **Ireland = "DPSP + RS nomination + President's election"** · **Canada = "Strong Centre, Residuary, Governor, Advisory SC"** · **Australia = "Concurrent + Joint sitting + Trade"**
- Preamble ideals: **Justice from Russia; Liberty, Equality, Fraternity from France** ("**R**-J, **F**-LEF")
- "**Procedure established by law**" = **Japan**. "**Due process**" = **USA**, and India does *not* use this phrase

### ⚠️ Traps
| Trap | Truth |
|---|---|
| "Residuary powers with the Centre: from the USA" | **Canada**. In the US, residuary powers lie with the **states** |
| "Concept of the Republic: from the USA" | **France** |
| "Fundamental Duties: from Japan" | **USSR** |
| "Rule of Law: from the USA" | **Britain** (A.V. Dicey) |
| "Joint sitting: from Britain" | **Australia** |
| "Indian judicial review is as wide as the American one" | **Narrower** in India ("procedure established by law"), though Maneka Gandhi (1978) brought in a due-process-like test |

## 2.3 The Preamble 📌📌

**Text (memorise the exact order):**
> WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a **SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC** and to secure to all its citizens:
> **JUSTICE**, social, economic and political;
> **LIBERTY** of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship;
> **EQUALITY** of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all
> **FRATERNITY** assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation;
> IN OUR CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do HEREBY ADOPT, ENACT AND GIVE TO OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION.

🧠 **Order hook: "SSSDR"** = "**S**ome **S**tudents **S**tudy **D**aily **R**egularly" (Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic)
🧠 **Liberty of "TEBFW"**: **T**hought, **E**xpression, **B**elief, **F**aith, **W**orship ("**T**he **E**lephant **B**rings **F**ruit **W**eekly")
🧠 **Fraternity assures 2 things**: **dignity of the individual** and **unity and integrity of the Nation** ("integrity" was added in 1976)

### Four ingredients of the Preamble
| Component | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| **Source of authority** | **People of India** |
| **Nature of the Indian State** | Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic |
| **Objectives** | Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity |
| **Date of adoption** | 26 November 1949 |

### Key words explained
| Word | Meaning in the Indian context | Exam point |
|---|---|---|
| **Sovereign** | Independent internally and externally; no external authority above it | **Commonwealth membership does not affect sovereignty**: it is a voluntary association, and the British monarch is only a "symbol of free association". India can acquire or cede territory |
| **Socialist** | **Democratic socialism**, not communist or state socialism. A **mixed economy** where public and private sectors coexist | The SC said Indian socialism is "a blend of Marxism and Gandhism, **leaning heavily towards Gandhian socialism**". Since the **1991 LPG reforms** the term has been diluted but remains as a welfare goal |
| **Secular** | **Positive** concept: all religions get equal status and support from the state (*Sarva Dharma Sambhava*). Scholars such as Rajeev Bhargava call it "principled distance" | The SC held secularism to be part of the **basic structure** (S.R. Bommai, 1994) |
| **Democratic** | Popular sovereignty; **indirect, representative** democracy, plus direct-democracy tools in gram sabhas. Covers **political, social and economic** democracy | Ambedkar: political democracy cannot last unless social democracy lies at its base |
| **Republic** | **Elected head of state** (the President, indirectly elected for 5 years). Political sovereignty lies with the people; no privileged class; all public offices open to every citizen | Britain is a democracy but **not a republic** (it has a hereditary monarch) |
| **Justice** | **Social**: equal treatment without discrimination. **Economic**: no discrimination based on economic factors. **Political**: equal political rights and access to office | **Social + economic + political justice = "distributive justice"**. From the **Russian Revolution (1917)** |
| **Liberty** | Not "licence". Subject to the limits laid down by the Constitution (reasonable restrictions) | From the **French Revolution** |
| **Equality** | **Civic** (Arts 14–18), **political** (Arts 325–326), **economic** (Art 39) | — |
| **Fraternity** | A sense of common brotherhood, promoted by **single citizenship** and the Fundamental Duty in Art 51A(e) | — |

### Quotes on the Preamble (quick recall)
| Person | Called the Preamble… |
|---|---|
| **N.A. Palkhivala** | "**Identity card** of the Constitution" |
| **K.M. Munshi** | "**Horoscope** of our sovereign democratic republic" |
| **Thakur Das Bhargava** | "The most precious part… the **soul** of the Constitution… a jewel" |

### Is the Preamble part of the Constitution? (Case evolution) 📌
| Case | Held |
|---|---|
| **Berubari Union (1960)** | The Preamble is a key to the minds of the makers, but **NOT part** of the Constitution |
| **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)** | **Overruled** that view: the Preamble **IS part** of the Constitution. It is of great importance and the Constitution should be read in its light |
| **LIC of India (1995)** | The Preamble is an **integral part** of the Constitution |
| **Dr Balram Singh / Subramanian Swamy v. UoI (25 Nov 2024)** | SC **dismissed petitions** challenging the insertion of "Socialist" and "Secular" (42nd Amendment). Parliament's **amending power under Art 368 extends to the Preamble**. The fact that the Preamble carries the adoption date of 26 Nov 1949 does **not** bar later amendment. Socialist means a welfare state (not a particular economic policy) and secular is part of the basic structure |

**Other points**
1. The Preamble is **neither a source of power** to the legislature **nor a prohibition** on its powers.
2. It is **non-justiciable**: its provisions cannot be enforced in courts.
3. It **can be amended** under Art 368, but **not its basic features** (Kesavananda).
4. It has been amended **only once**: the **42nd Amendment, 1976** added **Socialist, Secular, Integrity**.
5. Like the rest of the Constitution, the Preamble was **enacted after the CA had passed the rest of the Constitution**, so that it would match it.

| Trap | Truth |
|---|---|
| "The Preamble is a source of power to Parliament" | It is **not**. Powers come from specific provisions |
| "'Fraternity' was added by the 42nd Amendment" | Fraternity is **original**. Only Socialist, Secular and **Integrity** were added ("Unity" was original) |
| "The Preamble begins with 'We, the citizens of India'" | It is "**We, the People of India**" |
| "Secularism was not part of the Constitution before 1976" | The **word** was added in 1976. The **concept** was already present (Arts 25–30) |
| "The Preamble has been amended twice" | **Only once** (1976) |
| "Justice in the Preamble includes 'religious justice'" | Only **social, economic, political** |

# 📘 DAY 3 (Wed) – UNION & ITS TERRITORY (Arts 1–4) AND CITIZENSHIP (Arts 5–11)

## ⏱ Snapshot
- **Art 1**: "India, that is Bharat, shall be a **Union of States**." **Art 2**: admit or establish **new** states (outside the Union). **Art 3**: form new states or alter the areas, boundaries and names of **existing** states. **Art 4**: such laws are **not** constitutional amendments under Art 368, so a **simple majority** is enough.
- **Parliament can redraw India's political map without the states' consent** ("indestructible Union of destructible states").
- **Citizenship:** Part II (Arts 5–11) covers only people who were citizens at **commencement (26 Jan 1950)**. Everything after that is governed by the **Citizenship Act, 1955**, under **Art 11**.
- There are **5 ways to acquire** citizenship (birth, descent, registration, naturalisation, incorporation of territory) and **3 ways to lose** it (renunciation, termination, deprivation).

---

## 3.1 Union & Its Territory

### Article 1: two key points
1. **"Union of States", not "Federation of States"** (Ambedkar's explanation):
   - The Indian federation is **not the result of an agreement** among states, unlike the USA.
   - **States have no right to secede** from it.
2. **Territory of India** (wider) vs **Union of India** (narrower):

| Territory of India | Union of India |
|---|---|
| States + Union Territories + territories that may be acquired | **Only the States** (the members of the federal system that share powers with the Centre) |

Note: the **names of the states and UTs and their territorial extent** are in the **First Schedule**.

### Articles 2, 3, 4: compared
| | Art 2 | Art 3 |
|---|---|---|
| Deals with | **Admission/establishment** of new states **not part** of the Union | **Reorganisation** of **existing** states |
| Example | **Sikkim** (35th Amendment 1974 made it an "associate state"; **36th Amendment 1975** made it a full state) | Telangana (2014), J&K Reorganisation (2019) |

**Art 3 procedure 📌 (high-frequency)**
1. A Bill can be introduced in Parliament **only with the prior recommendation of the President**.
2. Before recommending, the President **refers the bill to the affected state legislature** for its views, within a specified period.
3. **The President (and Parliament) are NOT bound** by the state's views. They may accept or reject them, even if the views arrive in time.
4. A **fresh reference is not needed** every time an amendment to the bill is moved.
5. For **Union Territories**, **no reference** to any legislature is needed.
6. Under **Art 4**, such laws are **not amendments under Art 368**: they need only a **simple majority** and the ordinary legislative procedure.

### Cession of Indian territory to a foreign country 📌
| Case/Event | Point |
|---|---|
| **Berubari Union case (1960)** | Parliament's power under Art 3 **does not cover cession** of Indian territory to a foreign state. Cession needs a **constitutional amendment under Art 368**. This led to the **9th Amendment Act, 1960** (Berubari transfer to Pakistan; it was never actually implemented) |
| **SC (1969)** | Settling a **boundary dispute** does **not** need an amendment. It can be done by **executive action** because it involves no cession |
| **100th Amendment Act, 2015** | **Land Boundary Agreement (LBA) with Bangladesh**: India transferred **111 enclaves** to Bangladesh and received **51 enclaves**. India also gave up some adverse possessions. Amended the **First Schedule** for Assam, West Bengal, Meghalaya and Tripura |

## 3.2 Citizenship (Part II, Arts 5–11)

### Constitutional provisions (for people who were citizens at commencement)
| Art | Who became a citizen on 26 Jan 1950 |
|---|---|
| **5** | A person **domiciled in India** who (a) was born in India, **or** (b) either of whose parents was born in India, **or** (c) had been ordinarily resident in India for **5 years** immediately before commencement |
| **6** | A person who **migrated from Pakistan**, if they, either parent or any grandparent was born in undivided India and: (a) migrated **before 19 July 1948**: citizen if ordinarily resident since migration; (b) migrated **on/after 19 July 1948**: had to **register** (after 6 months' residence) |
| **7** | A person who **migrated to Pakistan after 1 March 1947** but later **returned under a resettlement permit** (6 months' residence, then registration) |
| **8** | A **person of Indian origin residing outside India**: could register with Indian **diplomatic or consular** representatives |
| **9** | A person who **voluntarily acquires** the citizenship of a foreign state is **not** an Indian citizen |
| **10** | Continuance of citizenship, **subject to** any law made by Parliament |
| **11** | **Parliament may regulate** citizenship by law. This is the basis of the **Citizenship Act, 1955** |

### Loss of Citizenship 📌
| Mode | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Renunciation** (voluntary) | A citizen of full age and capacity makes a declaration. ⚠️ If made **during a war** in which India is engaged, registration **can be withheld** by the Govt. **Minor children lose** citizenship too, but can **resume** it within 1 year of turning 18 |
| **Termination** (by operation of law) | **Voluntarily acquiring another country's citizenship** terminates Indian citizenship **automatically**. ⚠️ This does **not** apply during a war in which India is engaged |
| **Deprivation** (compulsory, by the Central Govt) | **Only for those who got citizenship by registration or naturalisation.** Grounds: obtained by **fraud**; **disloyalty** to the Constitution; **unlawfully trading or communicating with the enemy** during war; **imprisonment for 2 years** within 5 years of registration/naturalisation; **ordinarily resident outside India for 7 years continuously** |

🧠 Hook for loss: **"R-T-D" = "Resign (Renunciation), Transfer (Termination), Dismissed (Deprivation)"**

### Single Citizenship
- India has **single citizenship** (from Britain), unlike the US, where people hold both federal and state citizenship. All citizens enjoy the same rights regardless of their state.
- **Exception-like features:**
  - Parliament can prescribe **residence as a condition** for certain state jobs (**Art 16(3)**).
  - Art 15 does not bar states from giving **domicile** concessions in education (e.g., fee concessions).
  - Special provisions under the 5th/6th Schedules and Art 371 protect tribal and regional interests.
  - Erstwhile **Art 35A** (J&K "permanent residents") was **removed in 2019**.

### Rights available ONLY to citizens 📌 (not to foreigners)
| Category | Rights/Offices |
|---|---|
| **FRs** | **Art 15** (non-discrimination), **Art 16** (equality in public employment), **Art 19** (six freedoms), **Arts 29 & 30** (cultural and educational rights) |
| **Political** | Vote in Lok Sabha and state assembly elections (Art 326); become MP or MLA |
| **Offices** | President, Vice-President, SC and HC judges, Governor of a state, Attorney General, Advocate General |

### OCI (Overseas Citizen of India) 📌
- Introduced in **2005**. The **PIO card scheme merged into OCI in 2015**.

- **Not eligible:** a person who has ever been a citizen of **Pakistan or Bangladesh**, or whose parents/grandparents were.

# 📘 DAY 4 (Thu) – FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS I (Arts 12–24)

## ⏱ Snapshot
- **Part III (Arts 12–35)** is the "**Magna Carta of India**", inspired by the **US Bill of Rights**.
- Originally **7 FRs**. The **Right to Property** (Art 31) was **deleted by the 44th Amendment (1978)** and became a **legal right under Art 300A** (Part XII). **Now 6 FRs.**
- The FRs are **justiciable** (Art 32, Art 226) and **not absolute** (reasonable restrictions). During a National Emergency they can be suspended, **except Arts 20 and 21** (44th Amendment).
- Today's core: **Art 12** (definition of "State"), **Art 13** (judicial review), **Arts 14–18** (Equality), **Arts 19–22** (Freedom), **Arts 23–24** (against Exploitation).

## 4.1 The Six FRs: Map 🧠
| # | Right | Articles |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Right to Equality | 14–18 |
| 2 | Right to Freedom | 19–22 |
| 3 | Right against Exploitation | 23–24 |
| 4 | Right to Freedom of Religion | 25–28 |
| 5 | Cultural & Educational Rights | 29–30 |
| 6 | Right to Constitutional Remedies | 32 |

🧠 **"E-F-E-R-C-R" = "Every Fine Evening Rahul Cooks Rice"**

### Features of FRs 📌
1. **Some are for citizens only** (15, 16, 19, 29, 30); **others for all persons**, including foreigners and legal persons such as companies (14, 20, 21, 21A, 22, 23, 24, 25–28).
2. **Not absolute**: subject to reasonable restrictions.
3. Some are **negative** (they limit the State, e.g., Art 14, 18); some are **positive** (they confer privileges, e.g., Art 21A, 29, 30).
4. **Justiciable**: enforced by the SC (Art 32) and HCs (Art 226).
5. Parliament can **amend** them, but **not the basic structure**.
6. **Suspension:** During a **National Emergency** they can be suspended, **except Arts 20 and 21**.
   - **Art 19** is suspended **automatically** (Art 358), but **only** for an emergency declared on grounds of **war or external aggression**, **not armed rebellion**.
   - Other FRs are suspended by a **Presidential order** (Art 359).
7. **Restricted** by Arts 31A, 31B, 31C (saving of laws), Art 33 (armed forces) and Art 34 (martial law).
8. **Directly enforceable** in most cases. Some, like Arts 17 and 23, are backed by Parliament's law-making power under Art 35.

## 4.2 Art 12: Definition of "State" 📌
"State" includes:
1. Government and Parliament of India
2. Governments and legislatures of states
3. **All local authorities**: municipalities, panchayats, district boards, improvement trusts
4. **Other authorities**: statutory or non-statutory bodies such as **LIC, ONGC, SAIL**

**Test (Ramana Dayaram Shetty 1979; Ajay Hasia 1981):** a body is "State" if it is an **agency or instrumentality** of the government, judged by financial control, functional control, administrative control, monopoly status and public functions.
- **BCCI is NOT "State"** under Art 12 (*Zee Telefilms, 2005*), but it is amenable to **writs under Art 226** because it performs **public functions**.
- **Horizontal application:** *Kaushal Kishor v. State of UP* (**Jan 2023**, 5-judge bench, 4:1) held that FRs under **Arts 19 and 21 can be enforced even against private (non-State) actors**.

## 4.3 Art 13: Laws inconsistent with FRs are void 📌
- **Art 13(1):** Pre-constitutional laws inconsistent with FRs are void **to the extent of inconsistency** (**Doctrine of Eclipse**: such a law is "eclipsed", not dead, and can revive if the FR is amended).
- **Art 13(2):** The State shall not make laws that take away FRs. Such laws are void **ab initio**.
- **Doctrine of Severability:** only the offending part is struck down.
- **"Law" includes:** **ordinances, orders, bye-laws, rules, regulations, notifications, customs and usages** with the force of law. These can all be challenged.
- **Art 13(4)** (added by the **24th Amendment, 1971**): a **constitutional amendment is NOT "law"** under Art 13. However, **Kesavananda (1973)** held that an amendment can be challenged if it violates the **Basic Structure**.
- **Art 13 is the textual basis of judicial review** (along with Arts 32 and 226).

## 4.4 Right to Equality (Arts 14–18)

### Art 14: Equality before law AND Equal protection of laws (all persons)
| Equality before law | Equal protection of laws |
|---|---|
| **British** origin | **American** origin |
| **Negative** concept | **Positive** concept |
| No special privileges; all equally subject to ordinary law. Part of Dicey's **Rule of Law** | Equal treatment under equal circumstances. **Like should be treated alike** |

- **Reasonable classification is allowed** (not class legislation). Two conditions: (1) **intelligible differentia**, (2) **rational nexus** with the object.
- **Arbitrariness is antithetical to equality** (*E.P. Royappa, 1974*). **Manifest arbitrariness** is a ground to strike down laws (*Shayara Bano, 2017*: triple talaq).
- **Rule of Law** (Art 14) is part of the **basic structure**.
- **Exceptions to Art 14:**
  1. **Art 361**: the **President and Governors** are not answerable to any court for their official acts. **No criminal proceedings** can be instituted or continued during their term. Civil proceedings need **2 months' notice**.
  2. **Art 361A**: no liability for publishing **true reports** of Parliament/state legislature proceedings.
  3. **Arts 105/194**: MPs and MLAs are immune for anything said or any vote in the House. ⚠️ *Sita Soren v. UoI* (**March 2024**, 7-judge bench) **overruled P.V. Narasimha Rao (1998)**: there is **no immunity for bribery** (taking a bribe to vote or speak).
  4. **Art 31C** (laws for Art 39(b) and (c) exempt from Art 14 challenge).
  5. **Foreign sovereigns, ambassadors and diplomats**, and **UN agencies**, enjoy immunity.

### Art 15: Prohibition of discrimination (citizens only)
- **Grounds:** **only** religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth. *(Note: "descent" and "residence" are NOT in Art 15; they appear in Art 16.)*
- **Art 15(2):** access to **shops, restaurants, hotels, places of entertainment, wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads**. This applies **against private individuals too**.
- **Exceptions:**

| Clause | Provision | Added by |
|---|---|---|
| 15(3) | Special provisions for **women and children** | Original |
| 15(4) | Special provisions for **SEBCs, SCs, STs** | **1st Amendment 1951** (after *Champakam Dorairajan, 1951*) |
| 15(5) | **Reservation in educational institutions**, including **private** institutions (aided or unaided), **except minority institutions** | **93rd Amendment 2005** |
| 15(6) | **EWS** reservation up to **10%** in education | **103rd Amendment 2019** |

### Art 16: Equality of opportunity in public employment (citizens only)
- **Grounds:** religion, race, caste, sex, **descent**, place of birth, **residence**.
- **Exceptions:**

| Clause | Provision | Added by |
|---|---|---|
| 16(3) | **Parliament** may prescribe **residence** as a condition for certain state/UT jobs | Original (⚠️ only Parliament, not the state legislature) |
| 16(4) | Reservation for **backward classes** not adequately represented | Original |
| 16(4A) | Reservation in **promotion** for SC/ST, with **consequential seniority** | **77th (1995)**, **85th (2001)** |
| 16(4B) | **Carry forward** unfilled backlog vacancies, **outside the 50% ceiling** | **81st (2000)** |
| 16(5) | Office holders of a **religious or denominational institution** can be required to belong to that religion | Original |
| 16(6) | **EWS** up to **10%** in appointments | **103rd (2019)** |

### Key reservation cases 📌📌
| Case | Held |
|---|---|
| **Champakam Dorairajan (1951)** | Communal G.O. struck down, which led to the **1st Amendment** (Art 15(4)) |
| **Indra Sawhney / Mandal case (1992)** | • **27% OBC** reservation **upheld**<br>• **Creamy layer** to be excluded<br>• **50% ceiling** (except in extraordinary situations)<br>• **No reservation in promotions** (later overridden by the 77th Amendment)<br>• A **permanent statutory body** for backward classes (NCBC, given constitutional status by the **102nd Amendment 2018**, Art 338B) |
| **M. Nagaraj (2006)** | Upheld the 77th, 81st and 85th Amendments. Promotion reservation needs (a) **quantifiable data** on backwardness, (b) inadequacy of representation, (c) overall efficiency |
| **Jarnail Singh (2018)** | **No need for quantifiable data on backwardness** of SC/ST, but **creamy layer applies** to SC/ST in promotions |
| **Jaishri Patil / Maratha reservation (2021)** | Struck down Maratha quota (crossed 50%). Held that after the 102nd Amendment only the Centre could identify SEBCs. The **105th Amendment (2021)** **restored states' power** to make their own SEBC lists |
| **Janhit Abhiyan (Nov 2022)** | **103rd Amendment (EWS) upheld, 3:2**. Economic criteria do **not violate** the basic structure, and **the 50% ceiling is not inflexible** |
| **State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh (1 Aug 2024)** | 7-judge bench, **6:1**: **sub-classification of SCs and STs is permissible**. **Overruled E.V. Chinnaiah (2004)**. Several judges suggested a creamy layer for SC/ST too (not a binding direction) |

### Art 17: Abolition of untouchability
- "Untouchability" is **abolished** and its practice **forbidden in any form**. It is an **absolute** right with **no exceptions**.
- It is **not defined** in the Constitution or in the Act.
- **Laws:** Untouchability (Offences) Act **1955**, renamed the **Protection of Civil Rights Act (1976)**; the **SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act 1989**.
- Available **against private individuals**. The State has a **duty** to protect it.

### Art 18: Abolition of titles
1. The State **shall not confer titles**, **except military or academic distinctions**.
2. A **citizen** cannot accept a title from a **foreign State**.
3. A **foreigner** holding an office of profit or trust under the State needs the **President's consent** to accept a foreign title.
4. No citizen or foreigner holding an office of profit/trust shall accept any **present, emolument or office** from a foreign State **without the President's consent**.
- **Bharat Ratna and Padma awards are NOT titles** (*Balaji Raghavan, 1996*). They **should not be used as a prefix or suffix**; misuse can lead to forfeiture. *(Instituted 1954; discontinued 1977–80 under the Janata Govt.)*

## 4.5 Right to Freedom (Arts 19–22)

### Art 19: Six freedoms (citizens only) 📌📌
| Clause | Freedom | Grounds for reasonable restriction |
|---|---|---|
| 19(1)(a) | **Speech and expression** | 19(2): **sovereignty and integrity of India**, **security of the State**, **friendly relations with foreign States**, **public order**, **decency or morality**, **contempt of court**, **defamation**, **incitement to an offence** (8 grounds) |
| 19(1)(b) | **Assemble peaceably and without arms** | Sovereignty and integrity, public order |
| 19(1)(c) | **Form associations, unions or co-operative societies** ("co-operative societies" added by the 97th Amendment 2011) | Sovereignty and integrity, public order, morality |
| 19(1)(d) | **Move freely** throughout India | Interests of the general public, **protection of Scheduled Tribes** |
| 19(1)(e) | **Reside and settle** in any part of India | Same as (d) |
| 19(1)(g) | **Practise any profession, occupation, trade or business** | Interests of the general public; **professional/technical qualifications**; the **State carrying on any trade** (State monopoly) |

*(19(1)(f), the right to property, was deleted in 1978.)*

🧠 **19(2) grounds hook: "SIS-FPDCDI"**: "**S**overeignty, **I**ntegrity, **S**ecurity, **F**riendly relations, **P**ublic order, **D**ecency, **C**ontempt, **D**efamation, **I**ncitement"
- **1st Amendment (1951)** added "**public order**", "**friendly relations with foreign States**" and "**incitement to an offence**" (after *Romesh Thappar* and *Brij Bhushan*, 1950).
- **16th Amendment (1963)** added "**sovereignty and integrity of India**".

**Freedom of speech and expression includes (SC judgments):**
- Freedom of the **press**; **right to information**; right to **silence**; right to **fly the National Flag** (*Naveen Jindal, 2004*); **commercial advertisements**; right to **telecast**; right **against bandhs** called by political parties; right to **criticise** the government.
- Freedom of speech and trade over the **internet** is protected (*Anuradha Bhasin, Jan 2020*). Internet shutdowns must meet **proportionality** and be **reviewed periodically**.
- **Section 66A of the IT Act struck down** (*Shreya Singhal, 2015*) for vagueness.
- **Kaushal Kishor (2023)**: **no additional restrictions** beyond 19(2) can be imposed (for example on ministers' speech).
- **Sedition (IPC 124A)** was **put in abeyance** by the SC in **May 2022** (*S.G. Vombatkere*). The **BNS 2023** dropped "sedition", but **Section 152** penalises acts **endangering the sovereignty, unity and integrity** of India, which critics call "sedition by another name".

### Art 20: Protection in respect of conviction for offences (all persons, including companies) 📌
| Protection | Meaning | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| **No ex post facto law** | No conviction or greater penalty under a law that did not exist when the act was committed | Applies **only to criminal laws**, not civil or tax laws. The **trial procedure** can be retrospective. ⚠️ **Not available in preventive detention** |
| **No double jeopardy** | No person **prosecuted and punished** twice for the same offence | Only for proceedings before a **court or judicial tribunal**. **Departmental/administrative** proceedings are not covered |
| **No self-incrimination** | No person accused of an offence shall be **compelled to be a witness against himself** | Covers oral and documentary evidence. **Does NOT** cover compulsory production of **material objects, thumb impressions, specimen signatures, blood samples**. Applies **only to criminal** proceedings. *Selvi v. State of Karnataka (2010)*: **narco-analysis, polygraph and brain-mapping without consent** violate Arts 20(3) and 21 |

⚠️ **Art 20 and Art 21 cannot be suspended even in a National Emergency** (44th Amendment).

### Art 21: Protection of life and personal liberty (all persons) 📌📌📌
"No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to **procedure established by law**."

| Case | Development |
|---|---|
| **A.K. Gopalan (1950)** | Narrow view: protection only against **arbitrary executive** action, not legislative action. "Procedure established by law" taken literally |
| **Maneka Gandhi (1978)** | Overruled Gopalan's narrow view. The procedure must be **"right, just and fair"**, not arbitrary. Arts 14, 19 and 21 are linked (the **"golden triangle"**). This brought in **American "due process"** in substance |
| **K.S. Puttaswamy (24 Aug 2017)** | 9-judge bench: **Right to Privacy** is a FR (intrinsic to Art 21 and Part III). Overruled *M.P. Sharma (1954)* and *Kharak Singh (1962)* on this point |

**Rights read into Art 21 (high-yield list):** live with **human dignity** · decent **environment**, pollution-free water and air · **livelihood** (*Olga Tellis, 1985*) · **shelter** · **health** · free **education up to 14** (now Art 21A) · free **legal aid** · **speedy trial** · against **handcuffing** · against **delayed execution** · against **solitary confinement** · **fair trial** · **privacy** · **travel abroad** · **sleep** · **marry/choose a partner** (*Shafin Jahan/Hadiya, 2018*) · **die with dignity**: passive euthanasia and **living will** (*Common Cause, 2018*, procedure simplified in **2023**) · **sexual autonomy** (*Navtej Johar, 2018*: decriminalised consensual same-sex relations; *Joseph Shine, 2018*: adultery decriminalised) · **right against the adverse effects of climate change** (*M.K. Ranjitsinh v. UoI*, **March 2024**, Great Indian Bustard case, under Arts 14 and 21).

📰 **Links:** **Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023** (privacy follow-up), with the DPDP Rules notified in 2025 for phased implementation · *Supriyo Chakraborty (Oct 2023)*: SC **declined to legalise same-sex marriage**, saying it is for Parliament.

### Art 21A: Right to Education
- **86th Amendment (2002)**: free and compulsory education for children aged **6–14**, in a manner the State determines by law, which led to the **RTE Act 2009**.
- The same amendment: changed **Art 45** (DPSP) to **early childhood care and education for children below 6**, and added **Art 51A(k)** (FD: parents must provide educational opportunities).
- Earlier, *Unnikrishnan (1993)* read the right to education into Art 21.

### Art 22: Protection against arrest and detention 📌
| Punitive detention (ordinary law): rights | Preventive detention: safeguards |
|---|---|
| 1. **Informed of the grounds** of arrest<br>2. Consult and be defended by a **lawyer of choice**<br>3. **Produced before a magistrate within 24 hours** (excluding journey time)<br>4. **Released after 24 hours** unless the magistrate authorises further detention | 1. Detention cannot exceed **3 months** unless an **Advisory Board** (of HC-judge-qualified persons) reports sufficient cause<br>2. **Grounds communicated** to the detenu (facts against the public interest need not be disclosed)<br>3. Detenu gets an opportunity to make a **representation** |
| ⚠️ Not available to **enemy aliens** or to persons held under **preventive detention** laws | The **44th Amendment** reduced the period to **2 months**, but this provision has **not yet been brought into force** |

- **Legislative power over preventive detention:** **Parliament exclusively** for defence, foreign affairs, security of India. **Parliament and states concurrently** for security of a state, public order, maintenance of essential supplies and services.
- **Laws:** National Security Act **1980**, COFEPOSA **1974**, PSA (J&K); earlier MISA (1971), TADA, POTA.
- ⚠️ Laxmikanth: *no democratic country makes preventive detention an integral part of its constitution the way India does.*

## 4.6 Right against Exploitation (Arts 23–24)
| Art 23 | Art 24 |
|---|---|
| Prohibits **traffic in human beings**, **begar** (forced labour without payment) and other **forced labour** | **No child below 14** shall be employed in any **factory, mine or other hazardous activity** |
| Available to citizens **and** non-citizens; protects against the **State and private persons** | Does **not** prohibit harmless or innocent work |
| **Exception:** the State may impose **compulsory service for public purposes** (e.g., military or social service), without discrimination on **religion, race, caste or class** *(⚠️ "sex" is NOT in this list)* | **Child Labour (Prohibition & Regulation) Amendment Act 2016**: **complete ban** on employing children under 14 in **all** occupations (exceptions: helping in family enterprises after school hours, the audio-visual entertainment industry); **adolescents (14–18)** barred from hazardous work |
| **Laws:** Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act 1956, **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act 1976**, Minimum Wages Act 1948 and Equal Remuneration Act 1976 (both now subsumed in the **Code on Wages 2019**) | Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act 2005 (NCPCR) |
| *PUDR v. UoI (1982, the Asiad workers' case)*: paying **less than the minimum wage = forced labour** | *M.C. Mehta v. State of TN (1996)*: Child Labour Rehabilitation-cum-Welfare Fund |

# 📘 DAY 5 (Fri) – FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS II (Arts 25–35) & WRITS

## ⏱ Snapshot
- **Arts 25–28:** freedom of religion (individual and collective). **Arts 29–30:** cultural and educational rights (Art 30 is for **minorities only**).
- **Arts 31A, 31B, 31C:** "saving" clauses that **protect certain laws** from FR challenges.
- **Art 32:** "**heart and soul**" of the Constitution (Ambedkar). It is itself a FR and part of the **basic structure**.
- **5 writs:** Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto.
- **Arts 33–35:** Parliament's powers to restrict FRs for the armed forces, under martial law, and to legislate on FRs.

## 5.1 Right to Freedom of Religion (Arts 25–28) 📌
| Art | Content | Who |
|---|---|---|
| **25** | **Freedom of conscience** and the right to freely **profess, practise and propagate** religion. Subject to **public order, morality, health** and **other FRs**. The State may regulate **secular activities** (economic, financial, political) associated with religious practice; provide **social welfare and reform**; **throw open Hindu religious institutions** of a public character to **all classes and sections** of Hindus. **Explanations:** wearing and carrying the **kirpan** is part of Sikh religious profession; **"Hindus" includes Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists** for this purpose | **All persons** (individual right) |
| **26** | Freedom to **manage religious affairs**: every **religious denomination** may (a) establish and maintain institutions for religious and charitable purposes, (b) manage its own affairs in matters of religion, (c) own and acquire movable and immovable property, (d) administer that property **according to law**. Subject to **public order, morality, health** (⚠️ **not** "other FRs", unlike Art 25) | **Collective** right of **denominations** |
| **27** | No person shall be compelled to **pay taxes** for the promotion or maintenance of a particular religion. ⚠️ Prohibits **taxes**, not **fees** (a fee for a service, such as for pilgrims, is allowed) | All persons |
| **28** | **Religious instruction** in educational institutions: (a) **Wholly maintained** by State funds: **completely prohibited**; (b) **Administered by the State but established under an endowment/trust**: **permitted**; (c) **Recognised** by the State: permitted on a **voluntary** basis; (d) **Receiving State aid**: permitted on a **voluntary** basis | All persons |

🧠 Art 28 hook: "**Wholly-No, Trust-Yes, Recognised/Aided-Voluntary**"

**Key doctrines and cases**
- **Essential Religious Practices (ERP) test** (*Shirur Mutt, 1954*): only **essential** practices of a religion get Art 25/26 protection, and courts decide what is essential.
- **Propagation ≠ right to convert** others (*Rev. Stainislaus v. State of MP, 1977*). This upheld the state anti-conversion laws of MP and Odisha.
- *Shayara Bano (2017)*: instant **triple talaq** struck down, followed by the **Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act 2019**.
- *Indian Young Lawyers Association / Sabarimala (2018)*: women aged 10–50 allowed entry. The **review was referred to a 9-judge bench (2019)** on wider questions (ERP, the scope of Arts 25/26). **Pending.**
- *Hijab case (Oct 2022)*: **split verdict**, referred to a larger bench.

📰 **Current link:** **Waqf (Amendment) Act, 2025** (renamed the UMEED Act). Challenged under **Arts 14, 25, 26 and 300A**. In **Sept 2025** the SC **refused to stay the whole Act** but **stayed some provisions in the interim**. Keep tracking the final verdict. 🔗 Art 26 (right of denominations to administer property "**in accordance with law**").

## 5.2 Cultural & Educational Rights (Arts 29–30) 📌📌
| Art 29 | Art 30 |
|---|---|
| **Any section of citizens** with a distinct **language, script or culture** has the right to **conserve** it | **All minorities** (**religious or linguistic**) have the right to **establish and administer** educational institutions of their choice |
| No citizen shall be **denied admission** to a State-maintained or State-aided institution on grounds **only** of religion, race, caste or language | **Compensation** must be fixed so as not to restrict the right if the State acquires the property of a minority institution (44th Amendment) |
| Protects **minorities AND majority sections** too: Art 29 is not restricted to minorities | The State shall **not discriminate** in granting **aid** to minority institutions |
| | ⚠️ **Only minorities** (not "any section of citizens") |

- **"Minority" is NOT defined** in the Constitution.
- *T.M.A. Pai Foundation (2002)*: minority status is determined **at the state level** (the state is the unit, not the country).
- **Notified national minorities** (NCM Act 1992): **Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists, Parsis (Zoroastrians), and Jains (added 2014)**. That makes **6**.
- 📰 ***AMU case (8 Nov 2024)***, 7-judge bench, **4:3**: **overruled S. Azeez Basha (1967)**. An institution **established by statute** can still claim **minority status** if it was **established by a minority**. The test is who **founded it** ("genesis"), not who administers it. The **actual status of AMU** was left to a regular bench.

## 5.3 Saving of Certain Laws (Arts 31A, 31B, 31C) 📌
| Art | Added by | Protects | Against |
|---|---|---|---|
| **31A** | **1st Amendment 1951** | Laws on (a) **acquisition of estates**, (b) State management of property, (c) amalgamation of corporations, (d) changes in rights of directors/shareholders, (e) mining leases | **Arts 14 and 19** |
| **31B** | **1st Amendment 1951** | Acts in the **Ninth Schedule** (also created by the 1st Amendment; originally 13 Acts, now about 280+) | **All FRs** |
| **31C** | **25th Amendment 1971** | Laws giving effect to **Art 39(b) and (c)** (distribution of material resources, preventing concentration of wealth) | **Arts 14 and 19** |

- **Ninth Schedule and judicial review:** *I.R. Coelho (2007)*, 9-judge bench: laws placed in the Ninth Schedule **after 24 April 1973** (the Kesavananda date) **can be challenged** if they violate the **basic structure** (including the essence of Arts 14, 19, 20, 21). ⚠️ Tamil Nadu's **69% reservation law** is in the Ninth Schedule (76th Amendment, 1994).
- **Art 31C's journey:**
  - **Kesavananda (1973)** upheld the **first part** of 31C and **struck down the second part**, which said such a law could not be questioned in court for not giving effect to the policy.
  - The **42nd Amendment (1976)** extended 31C to **all** DPSP.
  - **Minerva Mills (1980)** **struck down** that extension.
- 📰 ***Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra* (5 Nov 2024)**, 9-judge bench, majority:
  - **Not every private resource** is a "**material resource of the community**" under Art 39(b).
  - It **rejected Justice Krishna Iyer's** wide view (in *Ranganath Reddy 1977*, followed in *Sanjeev Coke 1983*).
  - The relevant factors are the nature of the resource, its scarcity, its impact on the community, and its concentration.
  - It held that **Art 31C, as upheld in Kesavananda, survives**.

## 5.4 Right to Constitutional Remedies (Art 32) 📌📌
- **Ambedkar**: Art 32 is "**the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it**".
- The **SC is the "defender and guarantor"** of FRs. Art 32 is **itself a FR** and part of the **basic structure** (*L. Chandra Kumar, 1997*: judicial review under Arts 32 and 226 is basic structure).
- **Original but not exclusive:** the SC's power under Art 32 is concurrent with the HCs under Art 226. **Parliament can empower any other court** to issue writs (**Art 32(3)**), but **no such law has been made**.
- **Cannot be suspended** except as provided by the Constitution (Art 359 during a National Emergency).
- Only for **FRs**, not for other legal or constitutional rights (e.g., Art 300A property).

### Art 32 vs Art 226 📌
| Basis | Art 32 (SC) | Art 226 (HC) |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | **Only FRs** | FRs **and "for any other purpose"** (legal rights), so **wider** |
| Territorial reach | Throughout India | Within its territorial jurisdiction (or where the cause of action arises) |
| Nature | Itself a **FR**: the SC **cannot refuse** (in principle) | **Discretionary**: the HC may refuse |
| Suspension | Can be suspended during an Emergency (Art 359) | Not itself suspended, though FR enforcement under it is affected |

### The 5 Writs 📌📌📌 (very high frequency)
| Writ | Literal meaning | Purpose | Against | NOT against / when not issued |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Habeas Corpus** | "**To have the body** (of)" | Produce a detained person before the court and **release them if the detention is illegal** | **Public authorities AND private individuals** | When detention is **lawful**; detention for **contempt** of court/legislature; detention by a competent court; detention **outside the court's jurisdiction** |
| **Mandamus** | "**We command**" | Order a public official or body to **perform a legal (public) duty** they have refused or failed to perform | Public officials, public bodies, corporations, inferior courts, tribunals, govt | **Private individuals/bodies**; enforcing a **departmental instruction without statutory force**; **discretionary** duties; **contractual** obligations; the **President or Governors**; the **Chief Justice of a HC acting judicially** |
| **Prohibition** | "**To forbid**" | A higher court **stops** a lower court or tribunal from **exceeding its jurisdiction** or usurping jurisdiction (**preventive**: before the order is passed) | **Judicial and quasi-judicial** authorities only | **Administrative** authorities, **legislative** bodies, private individuals/bodies |
| **Certiorari** | "**To be certified**" or "to be informed" | A higher court **transfers** a case from a lower court or **quashes** its order (lack or excess of jurisdiction, or error of law). **Preventive and curative** | Judicial and quasi-judicial bodies. **Since 1991, also administrative authorities** that affect individual rights | **Legislative** bodies, private individuals/bodies |
| **Quo Warranto** | "**By what authority or warrant?**" | Inquire into the legality of a person's claim to a **public office**; prevents **illegal usurpation** | A **substantive public office** of a **permanent** nature created by **statute or the Constitution** | **Ministerial** offices, **private** offices |

🧠 **Memory hooks:**
- **Habeas Corpus = "Have the Body"**: the only writ that also runs against **private persons**.
- **Mandamus = "Must do"**: forces a duty.
- **Prohibition = "Pre"**: stops a case **before** it ends. **Certiorari = "Cure"**: quashes **after** the order (it can also act during proceedings).
- **Quo Warranto = "Who Warrants you?"**: any **interested person** can file, not only the aggrieved person.
- **Locus standi:** generally only the **aggrieved** person, **except** Habeas Corpus (any person on behalf of the detenu) and **Quo Warranto** (any interested person). PIL relaxed this generally.
- 📰 **ADM Jabalpur (1976)**, the "habeas corpus case": held that habeas corpus could not be sought during the Emergency. **Expressly overruled in Puttaswamy (2017)**.

## 5.5 Arts 33, 34, 35 📌
| Art | Provision | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| **33** | **Parliament** may restrict or abrogate the FRs of the **armed forces, paramilitary forces, police, intelligence agencies** and analogous forces | **Only Parliament**, not state legislatures. Such laws **cannot be challenged** for violating FRs (e.g., Army Act 1950) |
| **34** | Restrictions on FRs while **martial law** is in force in any area. **Parliament may indemnify** officials for acts done to restore order | **"Martial law" is not defined** in the Constitution |
| **35** | **Parliament alone** (not states) can make laws for **16(3)** (residence), **32(3)** (other courts' writ power), **33**, **34**, and to **punish acts declared offences under Part III** (untouchability under 17, trafficking under 23) | Ensures **uniformity** across India |

### Martial Law vs National Emergency
| Martial Law (Art 34) | National Emergency (Art 352) |
|---|---|
| Affects **only FRs** | Affects FRs, **Centre–state relations**, the distribution of revenue and legislative powers, the tenure of Parliament |
| Suspends the **government and ordinary courts** | Govt and courts **continue** |
| Imposed when **law and order breaks down** for any reason | Only on 3 grounds: **war, external aggression, armed rebellion** |
| Imposed in **specific areas** | Whole country or any part |
| **No specific provision** in the Constitution (implicit) | **Specific and detailed** provisions |

### Rights available against private individuals too 📌
**Art 15(2)** (access to public places) · **Art 17** (untouchability) · **Art 23** (trafficking, forced labour) · **Art 24** (child labour). Since **Kaushal Kishor (2023)**, also **Arts 19 and 21** (horizontally).

| Trap | Truth |

| "Mandamus can be issued against the President" | **No**. Mandamus does not lie against the President or Governors |
| "Prohibition can be issued against administrative authorities" | **No**, only judicial and quasi-judicial bodies |
| "Certiorari can never be issued against administrative bodies" | **Since 1991, it can**, where individual rights are affected |
| "Art 30 is available to all sections of citizens" | **Only minorities**. **Art 29** covers any section of citizens |

# 📘 DAY 6 (Sat) – DPSP, FUNDAMENTAL DUTIES, AMENDMENT, BASIC STRUCTURE

## ⏱ Snapshot
- **DPSP:** Part IV (**Arts 36–51**), from **Ireland**. **Non-justiciable** but "**fundamental in the governance**" (Art 37). Their aim is a **welfare state**, i.e., **economic and social democracy**.
- **Fundamental Duties:** Part IVA (**Art 51A**), added by the **42nd Amendment (1976)** on the **Swaran Singh Committee**'s recommendation. From the **USSR**. **11** duties (the 11th was added by the **86th Amendment, 2002**).
- **Amendment:** **Art 368**, Part XX, from **South Africa**. There are **3 types**: simple majority (outside Art 368), special majority, and special majority + ratification by **half the states**.
- **Basic Structure:** **Kesavananda Bharati (24 April 1973)**, 13 judges, **7:6**. Parliament can amend any part, but **not the basic structure**.

## 6.1 DPSP (Arts 36–51) 📌📌

### Features
- Resemble the "**Instrument of Instructions**" of the 1935 Act.
- **Granville Austin**: DPSP and FRs together are the "**Conscience of the Constitution**".
- Ambedkar: "**novel features**"; they embody "economic democracy".
- **Non-justiciable** (Art 37): not enforceable in courts, but courts **use them to interpret** and to judge the **reasonableness** of laws.
- **Art 36:** "State" has the same meaning as **Art 12**.
- They are **positive** instructions: they require the State to act.

### Classification (⚠️ not in the Constitution; it is the academic/Laxmikanth classification)
| Socialistic | Gandhian | Liberal-Intellectual |
|---|---|---|
| **38**: social order with **justice** (social, economic, political); **minimise inequalities in income, status, facilities** (38(2), **44th Amend. 1978**) | **40**: organise **village panchayats** | **44**: **Uniform Civil Code** |
| **39**: (a) adequate **livelihood**; (b) **material resources** distributed for the common good; (c) prevent **concentration of wealth**; (d) **equal pay for equal work** for men and women; (e) health of workers, children; (f) **healthy development of children** (42nd) | **43**: promote **cottage industries** on an individual or co-operative basis in **rural** areas | **45**: **early childhood care and education** for children **under 6** (86th, 2002) |

| Fundamental Rights | DPSP |
|---|---|

## 6.2 Fundamental Duties (Art 51A) 📌📌
**Background:** **Swaran Singh Committee (1976)** recommended **8** duties; the Govt added **10** via the **42nd Amendment**. The **86th Amendment (2002)** added the 11th. Source: the **erstwhile USSR** constitution. *(Japan is among the few democracies with a list of duties.)*

| Clause | Duty: every citizen shall… | 🧠 Keyword |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | **Abide by the Constitution** and respect its ideals and institutions, the **National Flag** and **National Anthem** | Constitution, Flag, Anthem |
| (b) | Cherish and follow the **noble ideals of the freedom struggle** | Freedom struggle |
| (c) | Uphold and protect the **sovereignty, unity and integrity** of India | SUI |
| (d) | **Defend the country** and render **national service** when called upon | Defend |
| (e) | Promote **harmony and common brotherhood** transcending religious, linguistic, regional or sectional diversities; **renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women** | Brotherhood + women |
| (f) | Value and preserve the rich **heritage of our composite culture** | Composite culture |
| (g) | Protect and improve the **natural environment** (forests, lakes, rivers, wildlife) and have **compassion for living creatures** | Environment |
| (h) | Develop **scientific temper, humanism** and the **spirit of inquiry and reform** | Scientific temper |
| (i) | Safeguard **public property** and **abjure violence** | Public property |
| (j) | Strive towards **excellence** in all spheres of individual and collective activity | Excellence |
| (k) | **Parent/guardian** to provide opportunities for **education** to their child or ward aged **6–14** (**86th, 2002**) | Education |

🧠 **Hook (a→k): "Constitution, Freedom, Sovereignty, Defend, Brotherhood, Culture, Environment, Science, Property, Excellence, Kids"** ("**C**an **F**reedom **S**urvive **D**espite **B**ad **C**ulture? **E**ducated **S**cientists **P**rotect **E**very **K**id")

### Features
- Apply **only to citizens** (not foreigners). Some FRs, by contrast, are for all persons.
- **Non-justiciable**: **no direct penalty** in the Constitution. Parliament can enforce them through law.
- Some are **moral** (cherish ideals of the freedom struggle) and some are **civic** (respect the Constitution, Flag, Anthem).
- They codify Indian tradition and values.

### Verma Committee (1999, Justice J.S. Verma), on operationalising FDs: laws that already enforce some FDs
- **Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act 1971** (Constitution, Flag, Anthem)
- **Protection of Civil Rights Act 1955** (caste and religion offences)
- **IPC (now BNS)** provisions on promoting enmity between groups
- **UAPA 1967** (declaring communal organisations unlawful)
- **Representation of the People Act 1951** (disqualifying MPs/MLAs for corrupt practices such as seeking votes on religious grounds)
- **Wildlife Protection Act 1972, Forest (Conservation) Act 1980**

### Criticism
1. **Not exhaustive**: important duties such as **casting a vote, paying taxes, family planning** are missing. *(The Swaran Singh Committee had recommended a duty to pay taxes; it was not included.)*
2. **Vague and ambiguous** ("noble ideals", "composite culture").
3. **Non-justiciable**, so critics call them a "code of moral precepts".
4. Placing them after Part IV (as an appendage) **reduces their value**.
5. Added during the **Emergency**, so their motives were suspected.

**Significance:** they remind citizens that **rights and duties are correlative**; they deter anti-national activity; they help courts judge the **constitutionality** of a law (*AIIMS Students Union, 2001*: FDs are as important as FRs).

## 6.3 Amendment of the Constitution (Art 368) 📌📌

### Three types of amendment
| Type | Majority | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Simple majority of Parliament** (⚠️ **outside Art 368**, so *not* counted as "amendments" for Art 368's purposes) | Ordinary legislative process | Admission/establishment of **new states** (Art 2); **formation, alteration of states** (Art 3); **creation/abolition of Legislative Councils** (Art 169); **Second Schedule** (emoluments of President, Governors, judges); **quorum** in Parliament; **salaries and allowances of MPs**; **rules of procedure**; **privileges of MPs**; use of **English** in Parliament; **number of puisne SC judges**; **more jurisdiction for the SC**; **official language**; **citizenship** (acquisition/termination); **elections** to Parliament and state legislatures; **delimitation**; **UTs**; **5th Schedule** (Scheduled Areas/Tribes); **6th Schedule** (tribal areas) |
| **2. Special majority of Parliament** | **Majority of the total membership** of each House **+ 2/3 of members present and voting** | **Fundamental Rights**, **DPSP**, and all other provisions not in categories 1 and 3 |
| **3. Special majority + ratification by half the states** (by **simple majority** in the state legislatures) | As in 2, plus ratification by **half the state legislatures** | **Federal provisions**: **election of the President** and its manner (Arts 54, 55); extent of the **executive power** of the Union and states (Arts 73, 162); **Supreme Court and High Courts**; **distribution of legislative powers** between the Union and states; **any of the lists in the 7th Schedule**; **representation of states in Parliament**; **power of Parliament to amend the Constitution** (Art 368 itself); **Art 241** (HCs for UTs); **Art 279A** (GST Council, via the 101st Amendment) |

🧠 Ratification hook: "**PEJ-LR-368**": **P**resident's election, **E**xecutive power, **J**udiciary (SC/HC), **L**ists & legislative relations, **R**epresentation in Parliament, Art **368** itself (plus GST Council)

### Procedure under Art 368 📌
1. Initiated **only in Parliament** (either House), **not in state legislatures**.
2. Moved by a **minister or a private member**. **No prior permission of the President** is needed.
3. Passed in **each House separately** by special majority.
4. **No joint sitting** if the Houses disagree. The bill **lapses**.
5. If it touches federal provisions, it must be **ratified by half the states** (simple majority), with **no time limit** prescribed.
6. The **President must give assent** (**24th Amendment, 1971**). He **can neither withhold assent nor return** the bill for reconsideration.
7. It then becomes a **Constitution (… Amendment) Act**.

### Criticism of the amending procedure
- **No special body** (like a Constitutional Convention) for amendments; Parliament holds most of the power.
- **States cannot initiate** amendments, except a resolution to create or abolish a Legislative Council (Art 169), which is outside Art 368.
- **Half the states** suffice for ratification, with **no time limit**.
- **No joint sitting** provision, leading to deadlock.
- Too **flexible**: 106 amendments in 75 years. The counter-argument is that this **adaptability** has kept the Constitution alive.

## 6.4 Basic Structure Doctrine 📌📌📌

### Evolution: memorise this chain
| Year | Case | Bench | Held |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1951** | *Shankari Prasad* | 5 | Parliament **can amend FRs** under Art 368; an amendment is **not "law"** under Art 13 |
| **1965** | *Sajjan Singh* | 5 | Same as Shankari Prasad |
| **1967** | ***Golaknath*** | **11** | Reversed: FRs are **"transcendental and immutable"**; **Parliament cannot amend FRs**; an amendment is "law" under Art 13. Applied **prospective overruling** |
| **1971** | **24th Amendment** | — | Parliament can amend **any part**, including FRs (Art 13(4), 368(3)); the President must assent |
| **1973 (24 Apr)** | ***Kesavananda Bharati*** | **13** (**7:6**) | **Upheld the 24th Amendment** (Parliament can amend FRs) and **overruled Golaknath**, **but** Parliament **cannot alter the "basic structure"** |
| **1975** | *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain* | 5 | **First application** of the doctrine: struck down **clause 4 of Art 329A** (39th Amendment), which put the PM's election beyond judicial scrutiny. **Free and fair elections** are basic structure |
| **1976** | **42nd Amendment** | — | Art 368(4), (5): **no limit** on amending power; no court can question an amendment |
| **1980** | ***Minerva Mills*** | 5 | Struck down 368(4) and (5). **Limited amending power** and the **balance between FRs and DPSP** are themselves basic structure |
| **1981** | *Waman Rao* | 5 | The doctrine applies to amendments made **after 24 April 1973** |
| **1994** | *S.R. Bommai* | 9 | **Secularism and federalism** are basic structure |
| **1997** | *L. Chandra Kumar* | 7 | **Judicial review under Arts 226/227 and 32** is basic structure |
| **2007** | *I.R. Coelho* | 9 | **Ninth Schedule** laws after 24-4-1973 are open to basic-structure review |
| **2015** | ***NJAC case*** (*SC Advocates-on-Record Assn.*) | 5 (**4:1**) | Struck down the **99th Amendment** (NJAC) for violating the **independence of the judiciary**. The **collegium** system continues |
| **2022** | *Janhit Abhiyan* | 5 (**3:2**) | 103rd Amendment (EWS) **does not** violate basic structure |

1. **Supremacy of the Constitution**
2. **Sovereign, democratic and republican** nature of the polity
3. **Secular** character
4. **Separation of powers** between legislature, executive and judiciary
5. **Federal** character
6. **Unity and integrity** of the nation
7. **Welfare state** (socio-economic justice)
8. **Judicial review**
9. **Freedom and dignity** of the individual
10. **Parliamentary system**
11. **Rule of law**
12. **Harmony and balance between FRs and DPSP**
13. **Principle of equality**
14. **Free and fair elections**
15. **Independence of the judiciary**
16. **Limited power of Parliament** to amend
17. **Effective access to justice**
18. **Principles (essence) underlying the FRs**
19. Powers of the SC under **Arts 32, 136, 141, 142**
20. Powers of the HCs under **Arts 226 and 227**

🧠 **Core 5 hook: "SFJ-SR"**: **S**ecularism, **F**ederalism, **J**udicial review, **S**eparation of powers, **R**ule of law

## Day 1
**Q1.1 1935 Act: "charter of slavery" yet borrowed**
- *Not a contradiction:* the Act was rejected for its **spirit** (no responsible government at the Centre, Governor's special powers, the princes' veto, communal electorates). Its **machinery** was borrowed: federal scheme, three lists, PSCs, Federal Court, Governor, emergency provisions.
- The framers **changed the source of authority** (from the British Parliament to "We, the People"), **added FRs, DPSP, universal adult franchise**, and **made the executive responsible** to the legislature.
- Practical reasons: continuity of administration, familiarity, and urgency after Partition.
- *Conclusion:* the Constitution borrowed the body but gave it a democratic soul. Ambedkar: "no shame in borrowing".

**Q1.2 Association → Representation → Responsibility → Autonomy**
- 1861 (non-official nominated Indians) → 1892 (indirect election) → 1909 (enlarged councils, but separate electorates) → 1919 (dyarchy, direct election) → 1935 (provincial autonomy).
- *Concession argument:* each step followed nationalist pressure: the 1885 INC, the Swadeshi movement (1905), WWI and the Home Rule movement, Non-Cooperation, Civil Disobedience.
- *Strategy argument:* separate electorates (1909, 1919, 1932); weightage to princes and loyalists in 1935; the central executive was never made responsible. These were "concessions without power".
- *Conclusion:* both. The reforms were **calibrated concessions** meant to co-opt moderates and divide the movement.

**Q1.3 Legitimacy of the CA**
- *Weakness:* indirectly elected, on a limited franchise (about 10–15% of adults), with nominated princely members.
- *Strength:* it reflected most groups; its debates were public; it adopted **universal adult franchise**; the first elections of 1951–52 effectively **ratified** the Constitution (people accepted it and voted under it); 75 years of acceptance (legitimacy through performance).
- *Conclusion:* procedural deficit, substantive legitimacy.

**Q1.4 Why citizenship and elections early?** To hold elections and constitute the provisional Parliament, you must first know **who is a citizen and voter**. The Election Commission (Art 324) and voter rolls had to be ready. Transitional provisions were needed so there was no vacuum on 26 Jan 1950.

**Q1.5 Communal electorate → Partition (chain)**
1909 separate electorate → politics organised along religious lines → 1916 Lucknow Pact (INC accepts separate electorates) → 1919/1935 extension → 1937 elections and the Congress–League rift → 1940 Lahore Resolution → 1946 elections fought on the Pakistan issue → Direct Action Day → 1947 Partition.

## Day 2
**Q2.1 "Bag of borrowings"**
- *Admit:* many sources (1935 Act, UK, US, Ireland, Canada…).
- *But adapted:*
  - **Parliamentary system with a written constitution and judicial review**. The UK has no written constitution.
  - **Federation with a strong Centre and integrated judiciary**. The US has a dual judiciary.
  - **Procedure established by law, expanded by Maneka** to a due-process-like standard.
  - **Positive secularism** in place of a US-style wall of separation.
  - **DPSP adapted to the welfare state**.
- Indigenous elements: Panchayats, special provisions for SC/ST, the 5th and 6th Schedules, Arts 370/371.
- Ambedkar's defence: a constitution should incorporate the best of humanity's experience.

**Q2.2 Federal or unitary?**
- *Federal:* dual polity, division of powers, a rigid amendment procedure for federal provisions, an independent judiciary, bicameralism; S.R. Bommai (federalism is basic structure); Finance Commissions.
- *Unitary:* Art 3, a single constitution, Governors appointed by the Centre, Art 356, all-India services, residuary powers, emergency provisions, the Rajya Sabha's unequal representation.
- *Recent:*
  - Governors delaying assent (TN 2025 verdict and the Presidential Reference)
  - The GST Council: **cooperative** but the Centre holds a ⅓ vote (states ⅔)
  - ONOE (129th Amendment Bill)
  - Disputes over agencies (CBI consent withdrawals)
  - Fiscal disputes over cesses and surcharges
- *Conclusion:* "**holding-together federation**" (Alfred Stepan); "cooperative-competitive federalism"; the balance depends on practice, not the text alone.

**Q2.3 Indian vs Western secularism**

| Western (US/France) | Indian |
|---|---|
| Strict separation (wall) | **Principled distance** |
| State does not intervene in religion | State may **intervene for reform** (Art 25(2), temple entry, triple talaq) |
| No state aid to religion | **Equal aid** to all religions (Arts 27, 30) |
| Individual-centric | Protects **individual and community** rights |

*Risk:* selective intervention (the reform agenda is applied unevenly across religions), the use of personal laws in vote-bank politics, and the debate over the ERP test. *Way forward:* constitutional morality and consistency.

**Q2.4 "Socialist" after 1991**
- The SC (Nov 2024) said "socialist" signifies a **welfare state** committed to equality of opportunity. It does **not** bind the state to any particular economic policy; an elected government can choose its economic model.
- Post-1991 evidence of the welfare commitment: NFSA, MGNREGA, DBT, Ayushman Bharat, PM-KISAN, progressive taxation.
- *Conclusion:* the meaning has shifted from **state ownership to welfare and redistribution**; it has not been lost.

**Q2.5 Preamble in interpretation:** Kesavananda (it helps identify the basic structure); S.R. Bommai (secularism); Maneka (fairness); the 2024 Preamble case. It works as an **interpretive aid** when wording is ambiguous.

**Q2.6 Republic and Commonwealth:** (1) Membership is **voluntary**, and India can leave at will. (2) The monarch is only a **symbolic head** of the association and holds no power over India. (3) India accepted membership **after** deciding to be a republic (London Declaration 1949). It is an extra-constitutional political arrangement.

## Day 3
**Q3.1 Indestructible Union of destructible states**
- *Explain:* under Art 3 Parliament can change states with a **simple majority**, and the state's views are **non-binding**. The Union itself cannot be broken: there is no secession right, and the Union is not the result of an agreement.
- *Why:* in 1947 there were 552+ princely states and Partition-era insecurity; India's diversity needed flexibility to reorganise.
- *Concerns:* J&K 2019 (a state downgraded to UTs without the elected assembly's views; the SC left this question open); the risk of majoritarian redrawing.
- *Way forward:* a convention of consulting states; requiring the **consent of the affected state** except in national-security cases (a debated reform); a role for an inter-state council.

**Q3.2 Criteria for new states**
- Language (1953–56) → ethnic/tribal identity (NE, 1963–87) → development and administrative efficiency (2000: Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Uttarakhand) → political and regional identity (Telangana 2014).
- *Future:* a **Second SRC**; criteria could include administrative viability, economic sustainability, regional backwardness, people's will, and fiscal capacity. Small states have mixed records (Uttarakhand vs Jharkhand).

**Q3.3 CAA and Art 14**
- *For:*
  - Intelligible differentia: persecuted religious minorities in **theocratic** neighbours.
  - Nexus with a humanitarian object.
  - Foreign policy and citizenship are Parliament's domain (Art 11); courts defer.
- *Against:*
  - Religion as the basis of classification touches secularism (basic structure).
  - Arbitrary exclusions: Ahmadiyya, Hazara, Rohingya, Tamils from Sri Lanka, Tibetans.
  - The "manifest arbitrariness" test (Shayara Bano).
- *Status:* challenges pending in the SC; Rules notified in 2024.
- *Balanced conclusion:* the humanitarian aim is legitimate; the classification needs principled, non-religious criteria (e.g., a persecution-based refugee law).

**Q3.4 Section 6A verdict (2024):** Humanitarian: it accommodated people displaced around 1971. Federal: it recognised Assam's special demographic concern through a cut-off date and detection mechanism. Fraternity: the majority held that the presence of different ethnic groups does not by itself violate the Art 29 right to conserve culture. *Concern (the dissent):* open-ended detection weakens enforcement.

**Q3.5 Deprivation only for naturalised/registered citizens:** Citizenship by **birth or descent** is a natural incident of belonging. Citizenship by registration or naturalisation is a **grant conditional on good faith and loyalty**, so a grant obtained by fraud or followed by disloyalty can be withdrawn. Taking citizenship away from people born as citizens could render them **stateless**, which goes against international norms (the 1961 Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness).

**Q3.6 Who decides citizenship?**

| Authority | Basis |
|---|---|
| **MHA / Central Govt** | Citizenship Act 1955 (grant, deprivation, CAA) |
| **Foreigners Tribunals** | Foreigners (Tribunals) Order 1964 under the Foreigners Act (now under the 2025 Act framework): decide whether a person is a foreigner (mainly in Assam) |
| **ECI / EROs** | Art 326 and RPA 1950: determine **eligibility to be a voter** (including citizenship) for roll purposes only, **not** a declaration of nationality |
| **Courts** | Judicial review of all the above |

*Debate:* the burden of proof, documents accepted, the risk of exclusion; the ECI's duty to ensure only citizens vote vs the right to vote.

## Day 4
**Q4.1 Art 21: creative constitutionalism or overreach?**
- *Creative:* fills gaps left by legislative inertia (Vishaka, privacy, environment, climate); makes rights meaningful for the poor (Olga Tellis, legal aid); transformative constitutionalism.
- *Overreach:* rights without resources (health and shelter are not enforceable in practice); policy-making by courts (judicially created guidelines); separation-of-powers concerns; uncertainty from an ever-expanding list.
- *Balance:* courts should **declare** rights and push the legislature (as in Puttaswamy → DPDP Act 2023). Judicial restraint where resources and policy are involved.

**Q4.2 Indra Sawhney → Davinder Singh**
- Indra Sawhney: creamy layer for OBCs, 50% ceiling. Nagaraj and Jarnail: creamy layer in SC/ST promotions. Janhit Abhiyan: economic criterion (EWS). Davinder Singh: **sub-classification within SC/ST**. Rohini Commission (OBC sub-categorisation, report submitted 2023).
- *Trend:* yes, from homogeneous categories to **intra-group equity**, so that benefits reach the most marginalised (e.g., Madigas, Valmikis).
- *Concerns:* the data deficit (hence the importance of the caste census); the political misuse of sub-classification; the creamy-layer debate for SC/ST, where untouchability-based exclusion is not purely economic.
- *Way forward:* evidence-based periodic review; a caste census (Census 2027).

**Q4.3 Formal vs substantive equality:** Formal equality treats unequals equally, which entrenches historical disadvantage. Art 14's reasonable classification, together with 15(4), 16(4) and 46, gives **substantive equality** (*N.M. Thomas 1976*: reservation is a facet of equality, not an exception to it). The EWS verdict extends this to economic disadvantage, but the dissent warned that it excludes SC/ST/OBC poor from EWS, which may run against equality's core.

**Q4.4 Preventive detention**
- *Necessary:* threats to security, terrorism, smuggling, and communal flashpoints where waiting for an offence is too late.
- *Safeguards:* Art 22(4)–(7), Advisory Boards, grounds communicated, representation; SC strictness (*Banka Sneha Sheela 2021*: PD cannot replace ordinary law).
- *Reality:* routine use (e.g., NSA, Goondas Acts) in law-and-order cases; Advisory Board scrutiny is often a formality; the 44th Amendment's 2-month limit has never been notified; NCRB data show thousands detained.
- *Reform:* notify the 44th Amendment provision; independent boards; time-bound review; compensation for illegal detention.

**Q4.5 Horizontal FRs (Kaushal Kishor)**
- *Helps:* (1) speech or privacy violations by large tech platforms or private employers; (2) private actors denying dignity or liberty, e.g., honour crimes, khap diktats.
- *Uncertainty:* (1) lack of clarity on which private actors and in what situations; (2) the courts' writ jurisdiction against private bodies is limited, so remedies are unclear and litigation may flood in.

**Q4.6 Section 152 BNS vs sedition**
- *Improved:* the word "sedition" and "disaffection towards Government" are gone; the focus is on acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity, which matches Art 19(2).
- *Concerns:* broad terms ("subversive activities", "encouraging feelings of separatist activities"), electronic communication covered, a higher maximum punishment (up to life). *Kedar Nath (1962)*'s "incitement to violence" threshold must still be read in.
- *Conclusion:* a cosmetic change unless the courts insist on a **proximate nexus to violence**.

## Day 5
**Q5.1 ERP test**
- *Problem:* judges deciding theology (Sabarimala: is celibacy of the deity essential? Hijab: is the hijab essential?); inconsistent results; it privileges orthodox versions over the lived practice of believers.
- *Alternatives:*
  - **Anti-exclusion / constitutional morality** (Chandrachud J. in Sabarimala): ask whether the practice excludes or degrades, not whether it is essential.
  - **Autonomy and sincere belief** tests.
  - Using **Arts 14, 15 and 17** as the limits.
- The 9-judge reference is pending. Note its significance.

**Q5.2 Art 32 and "go to the HC first"**
- *Dilution:* Art 32 is a FR and the SC cannot refuse it in principle (*Romesh Thappar 1950*).
- *Management:* pendency (80,000+ cases in the SC); HCs have a wider jurisdiction under Art 226; local facts are better heard locally; the SC still entertains urgent cases or those of national importance.
- *Conclusion:* acceptable as **self-restraint**, but it must not become a **blanket refusal**, especially in liberty cases.

**Q5.3 Ninth Schedule's journey:** 1951 (13 land-reform laws, to protect zamindari abolition) → 280+ laws including non-agrarian ones (e.g., MISA was once placed there; TN 69% reservation) → Waman Rao (1981): amendments after 24-4-1973 are open to review → **I.R. Coelho (2007)**: Ninth Schedule laws can be tested against the basic structure, including the essence of Arts 14, 19, 20, 21. *Conclusion:* it went from an absolute shield to a **qualified** one.

**Q5.4 Art 39(b) verdict (Nov 2024)**
- It rejected the reading that **all** private property is a community resource (Krishna Iyer).
- It ruled for a **context-specific test** (nature, scarcity, concentration, public trust).
- *Impact:* stronger property protection (alongside Art 300A); welfare legislation must now justify why a resource is a "material resource of the community"; it fits a market economy.
- *Concern:* it may constrain future redistributive laws, e.g., land, housing, natural resources.

**Q5.5 Writ against private detention:** **Habeas corpus**, which lies **even against private persons**. Courts: the **SC (Art 32)** or the **HC (Art 226)**. If the detainer is State police, the same writ applies and there is additionally a clear FR violation by the "State" (Arts 21, 22), plus potential compensation (*Rudul Sah 1983*, *Nilabati Behera 1993*). With a private detainer, a criminal remedy (wrongful confinement under BNS) is also available.

**Q5.6 Art 29 vs Art 30:** Art 29 is about **conserving** identity (language, script, culture), which matters to any group, including regional majorities that are minorities elsewhere. Art 30 gives minorities the extra protection of **establishing institutions**, a shield against majoritarian assimilation. Art 29 is **protective of culture**; Art 30 is an **affirmative right for vulnerable groups**.

## Day 6
**Q6.1 DPSP as a "cheque payable at the bank's convenience"**
- *Valid:* non-justiciable; slow progress (UCC, prohibition, the living wage, workers in management are unrealised; equal pay is still a gap).
- *Rebuttal (implementation record):*
  - Land reforms, the 73rd/74th Amendments, legal aid, MGNREGA, NFSA, RTE (Art 45 → 21A)
  - Environment laws; ICDS and POSHAN
  - Courts **read DPSP into FRs** (Unnikrishnan, Olga Tellis) and use them to test the reasonableness of laws
  - Political accountability at elections
- *Conclusion:* the cheque has been **encashed in instalments**. DPSP became the **agenda of governance**.

**Q6.2 Basic structure vs majority tyranny**
- *For:* it prevents a transient majority from destroying the core (the 39th and 42nd Amendments); it protects elections, the judiciary and rights; it has been adopted abroad (e.g., Bangladesh; the Kenyan courts' related doctrine; Pakistan discussed it).
- *Against:* not in the text; judges are unelected (the counter-majoritarian difficulty); the content is vague and open-ended; the NJAC verdict raised questions of judicial self-interest; the 2023 VP critique.
- *Balanced view:* it is a **"constitutional safety valve"**. Use it sparingly and articulate it clearly, with judicial accountability reforms (collegium transparency, MoP).

**Q6.3 Enforceable FDs?**
- *For:* rights and duties are correlative; some duties (environment, public property) can be enforced through specific laws (Verma Committee); civic culture.
- *Against:* vague terms become a tool for coercion; they shift the State's failures onto citizens; there is a risk of criminalising dissent ("respect ideals").
- *Middle path:* enforce through **specific, narrowly defined laws** plus education and awareness (NEP, NCC/NSS), not a blanket enforceability.

**Q6.4 Too flexible?**
- India: 106 amendments in 75 years. US: 27 in about 235 years (rigid; adapts through judicial interpretation).
- *Too flexible:* the 39th and 42nd show the dangers; frequent changes make the Constitution look "ordinary".
- *Defence:* a long, detailed text **needs** frequent technical changes (many concern details, e.g., salaries, schedules); the basic structure is a check on content; states ratify federal changes.
- *Conclusion:* flexible in procedure but **substantively rigid** because of the basic structure.

**Q6.5 UCC: Art 44 vs FRs**
- *For a UCC:* gender justice (Shah Bano 1985, Sarla Mudgal 1995, Shayara Bano 2017), national integration, secularism (laws based on religion vs equal citizenship), Art 44's mandate.
- *Concerns:* Arts 25–29 and minority identity; the 6th Schedule and tribal customary laws (Uttarakhand exempted Scheduled Tribes); the **21st Law Commission (2018)** said a UCC was "neither necessary nor desirable at this stage" and suggested reforming personal laws instead; the **22nd Law Commission** reopened consultation (2023).
- *State route:* Concurrent List Entry 5 (marriage, divorce, succession) allows states to legislate, but a patchwork of state codes undermines "**uniformity**" across India. Possible conflicts with central laws (Art 254).
- *Way forward:* **consensus-based, gender-just codification**; a step-by-step harmonisation of rights.

**Q6.6 NJAC: constitutional vs majoritarian democracy:** Even a unanimous political consensus cannot override **core values** (independence of the judiciary). Constitutional democracy = majority rule + limits that protect rights and institutions. *But note the counter-point:* the absence of judicial accountability in the collegium; the SC itself admitted the collegium needed improvement (the MoP issue is still unresolved).

**Q6.7 Art 48A + 51A(g) + 21 together:** Directive (State) + Duty (citizen) + Right (justiciable) form a **triad**. Cases:
- *Subhash Kumar (1991)*: pollution-free water and air under Art 21
- *M.C. Mehta* series: Ganga, Delhi air, the oleum gas leak (absolute liability)
- *T.N. Godavarman (1996– )*: forests
- *M.K. Ranjitsinh (2024)*: the right against the adverse effects of climate change

The NGT Act 2010 cites Art 21. 🔗 Environment, Week 11: Stockholm 1972 led to the 42nd Amendment adding Art 48A and 51A(g).
